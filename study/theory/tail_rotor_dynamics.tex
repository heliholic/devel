\large Thrust, Induced Inflow, and Inner-Loop Inversion}
\author{Dr.~Rudder \\ \small Rotorflight Project \\ \small \texttt{petri.t.j.mattila@gmail.com}}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent This note develops a control-oriented model of an RC helicopter tail rotor.
Starting from blade-element momentum theory (BEMT), we derive the thrust-versus-pitch
relation, add dynamic inflow via the Pitt--Peters first-order model, and invert the
coupled system to obtain the blade pitch required to track a commanded thrust. The
inverse is collected into a compact implementation-ready form for the inner control
loop. A linearisation about hover trim is also developed, exposing the lead--lag
structure of the plant and quantifying the inflow-softening factor that varies
across the operating envelope. The axial flow through the disc is shown to include
a body-yaw-rate contribution \(\ell_t\dot\psi\) that is of order the hover induced
velocity at RC 3D yaw rates and must be carried explicitly in the inverse.
Realistic parameter ranges for RC tail rotors are tabulated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

The tail rotor of a single-main-rotor helicopter is, in steady flight, a
near-quasi-steady thrust generator: its job is to balance the anti-torque of
the main rotor and, via the pilot's pedal input, to provide directional control.
On a radio-controlled (RC) model helicopter the same physics applies, but
the rotor operates at much lower Reynolds number, much higher rotational
speed, and with a single pitch input. From a flight-control perspective,
the relevant question is how the commanded blade pitch \(\thetat\) maps to the
thrust \(\Tt\) actually produced, in both steady and transient conditions.

Two effects make this map non-trivial. First, the thrust produced at a given
pitch depends on the induced velocity \(\vi\) through the rotor: as the rotor
generates lift, it accelerates air downwards through the disc, which changes
the local angle of attack of every blade element and reduces the effective
incidence. Second, the induced velocity itself takes a finite time to
develop, because the wake has inertia; the relationship between \(\vi\) and
\(\Tt\) is therefore dynamic, not algebraic. The combined effect is a
\emph{lead--lag} response in thrust to a step in pitch: an immediate
transient at the open-loop (no-inflow) gain, followed by partial relaxation
to a lower steady value as inflow builds up.

This note develops a compact, control-oriented model of these effects.
Section~\ref{sec:bemt} derives the blade-element thrust relation, an
expression linear in pitch and in induced velocity. Section~\ref{sec:inflow}
closes the loop on \(\vi\) using momentum theory and the first-order
Pitt--Peters dynamic-inflow model. Section~\ref{sec:inverse} inverts the
coupled system to obtain the pitch required to track a thrust command.
Section~\ref{sec:linear} develops the transfer function about hover trim,
exposing its lead--lag structure and quantifying the
\emph{inflow-softening factor} that varies across the operating envelope.
Section~\ref{sec:nondim} recasts the inverse in dimensionless form,
showing that every rotor and air parameter collapses into four
dimensionless coefficients and quantifying how those coefficients scale
with helicopter size. Section~\ref{sec:impl} collects the inverse into a
compact form suitable for direct implementation in an inner control loop. Section~\ref{sec:params}
tabulates parameter ranges for typical RC-helicopter classes and works a
representative numerical example.

\section{Blade Pitch and Rotor Thrust}\label{sec:bemt}
% =====================================================================

Section~\ref{sec:intro} set out the central problem: predict the thrust
\(\Tt\) that a tail rotor produces given the pitch \(\thetat\) applied
to its blades. This section builds the first half of that prediction---an
expression for thrust from the geometry and kinematics of a single
rotating blade, at a frozen instant in time, conditional on the induced
velocity \(\vi\) through the disc. The second half (computing \(\vi\)
itself) is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:inflow}.

The tool is \emph{Blade Element Theory} (BET), which decomposes the
rotor into thin radial strips, treats each strip as a two-dimensional
aerofoil section, and integrates the resulting elemental lift across
span and around the disc. Combined with momentum theory in
Section~\ref{sec:inflow} it becomes \emph{Blade Element Momentum
Theory} (BEMT). The blade-element half is purely kinematic and
aerodynamic; it does not invoke any global mass or momentum balance.
That is what makes it tractable as a standalone derivation.

\subsection{The Rotor and the Blade-Element Abstraction}\label{ssec:abstraction}

We model the tail rotor as \(N_b\) identical blades of length \(\Rt\)
and chord \(c\), rotating at constant angular speed \(\OmegaT\) about a
shaft, sweeping out a disc of area \(\At = \pi\Rt^2\). For an RC tail
rotor \(N_b\) is typically 2; for full-scale tail rotors it may be 3
or 4. The blades are set to a single pitch \(\thetat\) common to all
blades and to every azimuthal position. This is a meaningful
simplification specific to tail rotors: main rotors additionally vary
their pitch sinusoidally with azimuth (cyclic pitch) to control the
disc's tilt, but tail rotors normally do not---they have only one
degree of freedom, the blade pitch \(\thetat\), which acts as the
thrust command. (We avoid the aerodynamic term \emph{collective}
throughout: in RC helicopter usage ``collective'' refers exclusively
to the main rotor's pitch input, so this document uses ``pitch'' or
``blade pitch'' for the tail.)

The blade-element idea is to chop each blade into thin radial strips
of width \(dr\) at radial station \(r\), treat each strip as an
isolated two-dimensional aerofoil section that sees its own local
apparent wind, compute the elemental lift and drag on the strip from
2D aerofoil theory, then sum (integrate) over the span and multiply by
\(N_b\). The abstraction works because the spanwise extent of a strip
is large compared with the chord: lift on any given strip is dominated
by 2D mechanisms and depends only weakly on the flow at neighbouring
spanwise stations. The cost is that 3D effects---tip vortices,
trailing-edge wake roll-up, root flow distortion---are excluded by
construction and must be patched back in afterwards if needed (see
Section~\ref{ssec:bemt_assumptions}).

\subsection{Velocity Components at the Blade Element}\label{ssec:velocity_triangle}

At radial station \(r\) on the blade, two velocity components of the
apparent wind matter. The first is \emph{tangential}, lying in the
plane of the disc and pointing opposite to the blade's motion:
\begin{equation}
U_T \;=\; \OmegaT\,r,
\end{equation}
zero at the hub, linear in \(r\), peaking at \(\OmegaT\Rt\) at the
tip. This is what the blade sees just from rotating: the element is
moving forward through still air at speed \(\OmegaT r\), so the air
appears to move backward past it at the same speed.

The second is \emph{perpendicular}, normal to the disc plane, pointing
through the disc:
\begin{equation}
U_P \;=\; \vi \;+\; V_\infty.
\end{equation}
\(\vi\) is the induced velocity produced by the rotor itself
(present whenever the rotor produces thrust, drawn through the disc by
the pressure jump the blades create), and \(V_\infty\) is any external
axial component of the freestream---the air flow aligned with the
rotor shaft. Two physical sources contribute to it:
\begin{equation}
V_\infty \;=\; V_\infty^{\text{aero}} \;+\; \ell_t\,\dot\psi.
\label{eq:Vinf_composition}
\end{equation}
The first term, \(V_\infty^{\text{aero}}\), is the airframe-translation
contribution: for a tail rotor on a sideways-translating airframe, this
is the sideways translation along the rotor shaft. The second term is
the velocity of the tail-rotor hub through the air due to body yaw rate
\(\dot\psi\) about the airframe CG, with moment arm \(\ell_t\); the hub
moves in an arc whose tangent at the rotor is along the rotor shaft,
so this velocity is purely axial. The sign convention follows
\(\Tt\): \(\dot\psi>0\) when the airframe rotates in the same sense
the tail rotor's thrust is pushing it, so a sustained yaw in the
``with-thrust'' direction produces \(V_\infty>0\)---air fed into the
suction side of the disc, partially unloading the rotor. The
yaw-rate contribution is quantified in
Section~\ref{ssec:yaw_rate_axial} and shown there to be a first-order
effect at the yaw rates encountered in RC operation, not a small
correction.
In hover with no airframe motion, both contributions vanish and the
perpendicular flow is purely the rotor's own induced velocity. The two
notations \(V_\infty\) and \(\mu_z\OmegaT\Rt\) refer to the same quantity,
with \(\mu_z\) the non-dimensional form used in some textbooks.

\paragraph{The velocity triangle.}
Imagine looking edge-on at the blade element from outboard, so its
chord is perpendicular to the page. The element moves forward in the
disc plane at speed \(U_T\), and at the same time air is being drawn
through the disc at speed \(U_P\). In the element's frame these
combine into an apparent wind of magnitude
\(U = \sqrt{U_T^2 + U_P^2}\) blowing toward the element at an angle
\begin{equation}
\phi \;=\; \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{U_P}{U_T}\right)
       \;\approx\; \frac{U_P}{U_T}
\end{equation}
below the disc plane. \(\phi\) is the \emph{inflow angle}: the amount
by which the apparent wind is tilted out of the plane in which the
blade moves. Away from the root---where \(U_T\) is small and the
small-angle approximation fails---typical values are
\(U_P\sim\) a few m/s against \(U_T\sim\) tens of m/s, so
\(\phi\sim 0.05\)--\(0.15\,\)rad. Small-angle is justified across most
of the span.

\subsection{Effective Angle of Attack and Elemental Thrust}\label{ssec:alpha_dT}

The blade is set at geometric pitch \(\thetat\) relative to the disc
plane. But the section aerofoil cares only about its angle to the
\emph{apparent wind}, not to the disc. Since the apparent wind is
tilted down by \(\phi\), the angle that the chord line makes with the
flow---the \emph{effective angle of attack}---is reduced:
\begin{equation}
\alpha \;=\; \thetat \;-\; \phi.
\label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}
This is the central kinematic mechanism of this section. The geometric
pitch \(\thetat\) is what we command via the servo, but the element
only feels \(\alpha\), and induced inflow---through \(\phi\)---steals
incidence from the blade. Every newton of thrust the rotor produces
makes its own future thrust slightly harder to produce. This is what
the integrated result will quantify, and what the rest of the note must
contend with.

\paragraph{From angle of attack to elemental lift.}
Assume a linear two-dimensional lift curve,
\(C_\ell = a\,\alpha\), with slope \(a\). For an inviscid thin
symmetric aerofoil the slope is \(2\pi\) per radian
(\(\approx 6.28\,\text{rad}^{-1}\)); real aerofoils at typical RC tail
rotor Reynolds numbers give \(a\approx 5.5\)--\(5.9\,\text{rad}^{-1}\),
and we adopt \(a\approx \SI{5.7}{\per\radian}\) as a representative
value. The elemental lift on a strip of width \(dr\) at station \(r\)
is then
\begin{equation}
dL \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,U^2\,c\,C_\ell\,dr
   \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,(U_T^2+U_P^2)\,c\,a\,\alpha\,dr,
\end{equation}
acting perpendicular to the apparent wind.

\paragraph{From elemental lift to elemental thrust.}
Thrust on the rotor is the component of aerodynamic force along the
rotor axis, i.e.\ perpendicular to the disc plane. The lift vector
sits perpendicular to the apparent wind, which is itself tilted by
\(\phi\) below the disc plane, so the lift is tilted by \(\phi\)
\emph{away from} the rotor axis. There is also a drag vector aligned
with the apparent wind, contributing in the opposite sense. The exact
projection is
\begin{equation}
dT \;=\; dL\cos\phi \;-\; dD\sin\phi.
\end{equation}
For small \(\phi\), \(\cos\phi\approx 1\) (the lift projects almost
entirely onto the rotor axis), and \(\sin\phi\approx\phi\) so the drag
term is of order \(\phi\,C_d/C_\ell\) times the lift term---small,
since \(\phi\) is small and \(C_d\) is much less than \(C_\ell\) at
typical angles of attack. Dropping both corrections, and additionally
neglecting \(U_P^2\) against \(U_T^2\) in the apparent-wind magnitude
(consistent with \(\phi\) small), we obtain
\begin{equation}
dT \;\approx\; \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,(\OmegaT r)^2\,c\,a\,(\thetat-\phi)\,dr.
\label{eq:dT}
\end{equation}
Each small-angle simplification is itself \(O(\phi^2)\); collectively
they limit the validity of the derivation to a band of mostly small
inflow angles.

\subsection{Integration Across the Span}\label{ssec:integration}

Total rotor thrust is the elemental thrust \eqref{eq:dT} summed across
one blade's span and across all \(N_b\) blades:
\begin{equation}
\Tt \;=\; N_b\!\int_0^{\Rt} dT
     \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,a\,c\,N_b\,\OmegaT^2\!\int_0^{\Rt}\!
           (\thetat - \phi)\,r^2\,dr.
\end{equation}
Assume the induced velocity is \emph{uniform} across the disc---i.e.\
\(\vi\) does not vary with \(r\) or with azimuth. This is the standard
BEMT simplification. Under it, \(U_P\) is constant in \(r\), and
\(\phi(r) = U_P/(\OmegaT r) = (\vi+V_\infty)/(\OmegaT r)\) varies with
\(r\) only through its denominator. The integrand splits into two
pieces:
\begin{equation}
\int_0^{\Rt}\!\thetat\,r^2\,dr \;=\; \frac{\thetat\,\Rt^3}{3},
\qquad
\int_0^{\Rt}\!\phi(r)\,r^2\,dr
  \;=\; \frac{\vi+V_\infty}{\OmegaT}\,\frac{\Rt^2}{2}.
\end{equation}
Both pieces are tip-weighted---the pitch contribution as \(r^2\) and
the inflow loss as \(r\)---because elemental thrust scales with
dynamic pressure, which goes as \((\OmegaT r)^2\). Outboard stations
do most of the work; inboard stations are nearly irrelevant. This is
the reason rotors are conventionally analysed in terms of tip
quantities.

\subsection{Non-Dimensional Form}\label{ssec:nondim}

Substituting the two integrals back into the thrust expression and
collecting:
\begin{equation}
\Tt \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,a\,c\,N_b\,\OmegaT^2\!\left[
\frac{\thetat\,\Rt^3}{3}
- \frac{\vi+V_\infty}{\OmegaT}\,\frac{\Rt^2}{2}\right].
\end{equation}
Divide both sides by \(\rho\,\At\,(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\), the natural dynamic
scale (air density times disc area times tip-speed squared). Two
non-dimensional groups emerge:
\begin{equation}
\sigma \;\equiv\; \frac{N_b\,c}{\pi\Rt},
\qquad
\lambda \;\equiv\; \frac{\vi + V_\infty}{\OmegaT\Rt}.
\end{equation}
\(\sigma\) is the \emph{rotor solidity}: the fraction of the disc's
swept area that is actually covered by blade planform. It is
``how much rotor there is per unit disc area.'' Typical RC tail
rotors have \(\sigma\approx 0.10\)--\(0.20\), somewhat higher than
main rotors (\(\sigma\approx 0.05\)--\(0.10\)) because tail rotors
are short-radius and need proportionally more blade chord to
generate their share of thrust. \(\lambda\) is the \emph{inflow
ratio}: the through-disc velocity expressed as a fraction of tip
speed. It is small (\(\sim 0.05\)--\(0.15\) in hover) because the
tip moves much faster than the wake.

The thrust coefficient
\begin{equation}
C_T \;\equiv\; \frac{\Tt}{\rho\,\At\,(\OmegaT\Rt)^2}
\end{equation}
is thrust non-dimensionalised by the same scale---a kind of
``thrust per unit tip dynamic pressure per unit disc area.''
Substituting these groups gives the standard BEMT result:
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
C_T \;=\; \frac{\sigma a}{2}\!\left(\frac{\thetat}{3}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right).
\label{eq:CT_BEMT}
\end{empheq}
The factor \(1/3\) on \(\thetat\) is the \(r^2\) weighting of the
pitch contribution; the factor \(1/2\) on \(\lambda\) is the \(r\)
weighting of the inflow loss. Dimensional thrust is recovered as
\begin{equation}
\Tt \;=\; C_T\,\rho\,\At\,(\OmegaT\Rt)^2.
\label{eq:T_from_CT}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dimensional Form}\label{ssec:dimensional}

Equation~\eqref{eq:CT_BEMT} expresses thrust through the dimensionless
coefficient \(C_T\). For the rest of this note it is more convenient to
carry thrust in newtons directly. Multiplying through by the natural
dimensional scale \(\rho\At(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\), and writing the inflow
ratio in expanded form \(\lambda = (\vi+V_\infty)/(\OmegaT\Rt)\):
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\Tt \;=\; \rho\At(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\;\frac{\sigma a}{2}\!
\left(\frac{\thetat}{3} \;-\; \frac{\vi + V_\infty}{2\,\OmegaT\Rt}\right).
\label{eq:linT}
\end{empheq}
At fixed \(\OmegaT\) (held by the main-rotor governor on an RC
helicopter), the prefactor and the aerodynamic group \(\sigma a\) are
constants of the rotor: they depend on geometry (\(\Rt\), \(c\),
\(N_b\)), air density, lift-curve slope, and tip speed, but not on the
operating point. \emph{Equation~\eqref{eq:linT} is exact within the BEMT
assumptions listed below}; it is not a linearisation about any
particular point---the linearity in \(\thetat\), \(\vi\), and
\(V_\infty\) is a property of the underlying derivation.

\paragraph{Reading the two terms.} The first term, proportional to
\(\thetat/3\), is the thrust the rotor would produce per radian of
blade pitch if no induced inflow developed: the open-loop or
``frozen-wake'' pitch effectiveness, scaling as \((\OmegaT\Rt)^2\) (it
is dynamic-pressure-like). The second, proportional to
\((\vi+V_\infty)/(2\OmegaT\Rt)\), is the rate at which thrust is given
back as inflow builds: each m/s of through-disc flow costs a
proportional amount of thrust, scaling only as \(\OmegaT\Rt\) (it is
velocity-like). The ratio of inflow penalty to pitch effectiveness is
therefore purely kinematic, set by the factor
\begin{equation}
\frac{3}{2\,\OmegaT\Rt},
\label{eq:K2_K1_ratio}
\end{equation}
which is independent of blade geometry, density, lift-curve slope, or
solidity, and which reappears as a structural feature in the inverse
(Section~\ref{sec:inverse}) and in the dimensionless pitch law of
Section~\ref{sec:nondim}. Faster-spinning rotors are less penalised by
inflow, in the precise sense that \eqref{eq:K2_K1_ratio} is smaller.
The rest of this note is, in one sense, a careful study of how the
second term in \eqref{eq:linT} evolves dynamically.

\subsection{Assumptions and Where Each Is Repaired}\label{ssec:bemt_assumptions}

Equation \eqref{eq:linT}, and the BEMT result \eqref{eq:CT_BEMT} from
which it descends, rest on the following simplifications:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Uniform induced velocity \(\vi\) across the disc.}
      Repaired in Section~\ref{sec:inflow} via momentum theory, which
      \emph{supplies} the value of \(\vi\) given the thrust. The
      one-state Pitt--Peters extension makes \(\vi\) dynamic.
\item \emph{Small inflow angle, \(\phi\ll 1\)}, justifying
      \(\tan\phi\approx\phi\), \(\cos\phi\approx 1\), and
      \(U_P^2\ll U_T^2\). Holds across most of the span but breaks
      down near the root, where \(U_T\) is small; root cutout
      mitigates this.
\item \emph{Linear two-dimensional lift curve}, \(C_\ell=a\alpha\),
      up to stall. Stall, Reynolds-number effects, and Mach-number
      effects (less relevant for RC) require empirical or
      table-lookup corrections to \(a\).
\item \emph{Rectangular, untwisted blade with constant chord \(c\).}
      Non-rectangular or twisted blades introduce \(r\)-dependent
      factors inside the span integrals but no change to the basic
      structure.
\item \emph{No tip loss and no root cutout} (integration from
      \(0\) to \(\Rt\)). Tip loss is conventionally handled by
      multiplying \(C_T\) by a Prandtl tip-loss factor
      \(B\approx 0.97\); root cutout by truncating the lower
      integration limit.
\item \emph{Thrust contribution from section drag is ignored}
      (\(O(\phi^2)\), see Section~\ref{ssec:alpha_dT}).
\item \emph{Constant rotor speed} \(\OmegaT\), held by the
      governor.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:params} quantifies where each of these breaks down
for RC-scale tail rotors. The first item---uniform inflow---is the
load-bearing one for what follows: the rest of the document is, in a
sense, a careful study of what happens once \(\vi\) is allowed its
own dynamics.

\section{Induced Inflow: Momentum Theory}\label{sec:inflow}
% =====================================================================

The blade-element derivation of Section~\ref{sec:bemt} produced
\(C_T = (\sigma a/2)(\thetat/3 - \lambda/2)\), which contains \emph{two}
unknowns: the pitch \(\thetat\) (which we set) and the inflow ratio
\(\lambda\) (which we do not). To close the system we need a second
relationship between thrust and induced velocity. \emph{Momentum theory}
supplies it by treating the rotor disc as a whole and applying conservation
of mass and momentum to the column of air passing through it.

For this section take positive velocity to mean flow in the thrust-producing
direction through the rotor. Thus \(V_\infty\) is the already-existing axial
flow through the disc, \(\vi\) is the extra velocity induced by the rotor, and
\(V_\infty+\vi\) is the through-disc velocity seen by the actuator disc. The
theory below describes the normal working state of the rotor, with a smooth
wake; strongly reversed flow, vortex-ring operation, and separated blade
aerodynamics are outside this ideal model.

\subsection{The Actuator Disc and the Mass-Flow Argument}\label{ssec:actuator_disc}

Replace the physical rotor by an idealised \emph{actuator disc}: an
infinitesimally thin permeable surface of area \(\At\) that imparts a
uniform pressure jump \(\Delta p\) to the air passing through it. The
disc does no modelling of individual blades; it is only a bookkeeping
surface that adds energy to the flow. In the ideal actuator-disc model
there is no swirl, no profile loss, and no non-uniform loading.

Draw a streamtube enclosing the disc. Station~1 is far upstream, where
the pressure is ambient and the axial velocity is \(V_\infty\). Stations
2 and 3 are immediately on the upstream and downstream faces of the disc;
the velocity is continuous through the disc, so both sides have axial
velocity \(V_\infty+\vi\), but the pressure jumps by \(\Delta p\).
Station~4 is far downstream, where the pressure has returned to ambient
and the axial velocity is \(w_\infty\). In hover \(V_\infty=0\), so the
upstream flow starts from rest and the streamtube contracts as the rotor
pulls air through it.

Three relationships govern this column of air:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Mass conservation.} The mass flow rate
      \(\dot{m}=\rho A(x)U(x)\) is the same at every cross-section of the
      streamtube. The area \(A(x)\) changes as the streamtube contracts,
      so only at the disc can we write the mass flow as
      \begin{equation}
      \dot{m} \;=\; \rho\At\,(V_\infty+\vi),
      \label{eq:mdot_disc}
      \end{equation}
      where \(\vi\) is the induced velocity \emph{at the disc}.
\item \emph{Momentum.} The thrust on the rotor is the reaction to the
      change in axial momentum of the air, evaluated between far upstream
      and far downstream:
      \(\Tt = \dot{m}\,(w_\infty - V_\infty)\), where \(w_\infty\) is
      the axial velocity in the far wake.
\item \emph{Bernoulli, separately above and below the disc.} Energy is
      conserved along streamlines on each side of the disc, but \emph{not}
      across it, because the rotor does work on the fluid. Applying
      Bernoulli from station~1 to station~2 and from station~3 to
      station~4 gives
      \begin{align}
      p_\infty + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V_\infty^2
        &= p_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho (V_\infty+\vi)^2,\\
      p_3 + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho (V_\infty+\vi)^2
        &= p_\infty + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho w_\infty^2.
      \end{align}
      The pressure jump is \(\Delta p=p_3-p_2\), and the disc force is
      \(\Tt=\Delta p\,\At\). Subtracting the two Bernoulli equations gives
      \begin{equation}
      \frac{\Tt}{\At}
      \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\rho\,(w_\infty^2 - V_\infty^2).
      \label{eq:pressure_jump}
      \end{equation}
      Combining \eqref{eq:pressure_jump} with the momentum equation and
      \eqref{eq:mdot_disc} gives the celebrated \emph{Froude result}:
      \begin{equation}
      w_\infty \;=\; V_\infty + 2\,\vi.
      \label{eq:froude}
      \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The Froude result is non-obvious and worth dwelling on: \emph{half} the
total velocity increment \(w_\infty-V_\infty\) has already appeared at
the disc, and the other half is added downstream. In hover this means the
far wake moves at \(2\vi\); by mass conservation its area is then
\(\At/2\). This factor of two appears in every momentum-theory thrust
expression and is the reason rotors are \emph{efficient} thrust generators
at low disc loading: they create thrust by moving a large mass of air by a
moderate speed, rather than a small mass of air by a very high speed.

Substituting \(w_\infty - V_\infty = 2\vi\) into the momentum equation,
together with \(\dot{m} = \rho\At(V_\infty+\vi)\), gives the
\emph{axial-flight} form of the momentum balance:
\begin{equation}
\Tt \;=\; 2\,\rho\,\At\,\vi\,(V_\infty+\vi).
\label{eq:momentum_axial}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Edgewise Flow: The Full Momentum Balance}\label{ssec:edgewise_flow}

For a tail rotor mounted on a translating airframe, the free-stream is
not generally aligned with the rotor axis. Decompose the airframe
velocity at the rotor into a component along the rotor axis (axial,
\(V_\infty\), which feeds the streamtube directly) and a component in
the disc plane (edgewise, \(V_\perp\), which sweeps air sideways through
the disc). A useful way to picture this is as a tilted streamtube: the
wake is still accelerated mainly in the rotor-axis direction, but fresh
air is also swept through the disc from the side. That extra sweeping
increases the mass flow through the actuator disc, so for a given thrust
the required induced velocity is smaller than it would be in pure hover.

Glauert's approximation captures that effect by replacing the axial
disc velocity in the mass-flow estimate with the resultant speed at the
disc: the vector sum of the in-plane sweep \(V_\perp\) and the
through-disc flow \(V_\infty + \vi\):
\begin{equation}
U_{\text{disc}} \;=\; \sqrt{(V_\infty+\vi)^2 + V_\perp^2}.
\end{equation}
The mass-flow rate through the disc is then modelled as
\(\dot{m} = \rho\At\,U_{\text{disc}}\), and the axial momentum imparted
to the flow remains \(2\vi\), as in the purely axial case. This is not a
new exact Bernoulli derivation; it is the standard control-oriented
momentum closure used for oblique inflow. The momentum equation becomes
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\Tt \;=\; 2\,\rho\,\At\,\vi\,\sqrt{(V_\infty+\vi)^2 + V_\perp^2}.
\label{eq:momentum}
\end{empheq}
The square-root factor is just the resultant velocity at the disc; the
factor of two is Froude. In the purely axial case (\(V_\perp=0\)) we
recover \eqref{eq:momentum_axial}, and in the hover limit
(\(V_\infty=V_\perp=0\)) the relation simplifies further to
\begin{equation}
\Tt \;=\; 2\,\rho\,\At\,\vi^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
v_h \;\equiv\; \sqrt{\frac{\Tt}{2\,\rho\,\At}}.
\label{eq:vh}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Reading the hover result.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:vh} is the single most-cited result of rotor
aerodynamics. It says induced velocity is set by \emph{disc loading}
\(\Tt/\At\): rotors with larger disc area need less induced velocity for
the same thrust, and induced power \(\Tt\,v_h \propto \Tt^{3/2}/\sqrt{\At}\)
is lower as a result. A tail rotor (small \(\At\), high \(\Tt/\At\))
therefore has \emph{high} \(\vi\) compared with a main rotor of equal
thrust---a numerical example in Section~\ref{sec:params} gives
\(v_h\sim\SI{9}{\metre\per\second}\) for a typical 700 class tail rotor
producing 12\,N of thrust.

\subsection{Yaw Rate as a Source of Axial Flow}\label{ssec:yaw_rate_axial}

The free-stream axial velocity \(V_\infty\) introduced in
Section~\ref{ssec:velocity_triangle} has two sources, written together
in~\eqref{eq:Vinf_composition} as \(V_\infty=V_\infty^{\text{aero}}+\ell_t\dot\psi\).
The first is straightforward airframe translation along the rotor
shaft. The second deserves a closer look because it is specific to
yawing flight and is large enough at RC yaw rates to dominate the
axial-flow contribution.

When the airframe yaws at rate \(\dot\psi\) about a vertical axis
through its centre of mass, the tail rotor hub---located at distance
\(\ell_t\) behind the CG---swings through an arc at tangential
velocity \(\ell_t\,\dot\psi\). Because the tail boom is normally
perpendicular to the rotor shaft, the tangent to that arc at the rotor
hub is aligned with the shaft. The yaw-induced hub motion is therefore
axial through the disc, not edgewise, and it enters the momentum
balance through \(V_\infty\) rather than \(V_\perp\):
\begin{equation}
V_\infty \;=\; V_\infty^{\text{aero}} \;+\; \ell_t\,\dot\psi,
\qquad
V_\perp \;=\; V_\perp^{\text{aero}}
\quad\text{(no yaw-rate contribution).}
\label{eq:Vinf_yaw_decomposition}
\end{equation}
The sign convention is fixed by demanding self-consistency with the
thrust direction. In the document's convention \(V_\infty>0\) means flow
into the rotor in the same direction as the wake it produces, so
\(\dot\psi>0\) corresponds to airframe rotation in the same sense the
tail-rotor thrust is pushing it---a ``with-thrust'' pirouette. In that
case the rotor is moving \emph{into} its own wake direction; air is fed
into the suction side of the disc; and the rotor needs less of its own
induced velocity to deliver a given thrust. An ``against-thrust''
pirouette gives \(\dot\psi<0\) and pushes the operating point toward
the windmill-brake or vortex-ring states where the smooth-wake
momentum closure breaks down (Section~\ref{ssec:actuator_disc}).

\paragraph{Magnitude.}
RC helicopters routinely reach yaw rates of \(\dot\psi=\SI{600}{\degree\per\second}
\approx \SI{10.5}{\radian\per\second}\) in 3D flight, with extreme
pirouette flips going higher. For a 700 class machine with
\(\ell_t\approx\SI{0.95}{\metre}\) the resulting axial velocity is
\(\ell_t\dot\psi\approx\SI{10}{\metre\per\second}\), comparable to or
greater than the hover induced velocity \(\bvi\approx\SI{9}{\metre\per\second}\)
of the same rotor (see Section~\ref{ssec:worked_example}). Smaller
classes get smaller \(\ell_t\) but proportionally smaller \(\bvi\) too,
so the dimensionless ratio \(\ell_t\dot\psi/\bvi\) reaches order unity
across the size range. The non-dimensional axial inflow
\(\mu_z=\ell_t\dot\psi/(\OmegaT\Rt)\) is then of order
\(\lambda_h\approx 0.06\) or larger---the same order as the inflow
ratio the rotor produces on its own. The yaw-rate contribution is
therefore a first-order effect on the momentum balance, not a
perturbation.

\paragraph{What survives, what changes.}
The momentum equation \eqref{eq:momentum} is already written in terms of
the composite \(V_\infty\); using \eqref{eq:Vinf_yaw_decomposition} to
include yaw rate requires no change of form. Likewise the BEMT thrust
law \eqref{eq:linT} carries \(V_\infty\) symbolically and is unchanged
in form by the yaw-rate contribution. What \emph{is} changed is the
operating point: the trim is no longer the hover trim \(V_\infty=0\)
unless \(\dot\psi=0\), so the hover-specialised forms
(eqs.~\eqref{eq:vh}, \eqref{eq:lambda_implicit}, and the hover
linearisation of Section~\ref{sec:linear}) no longer apply during a
sustained pirouette. The full axial or edgewise momentum closure must
be used.

\paragraph{Effects not captured here.}
Three further consequences of yaw rate are second order at RC
amplitudes and are not modelled in this note. (i) The hub-motion
component also varies azimuthally with each blade's position---the
classic ``advancing/retreating'' asymmetry of edgewise flight---but
for pure yaw the hub motion is along the shaft, so this asymmetry is
small. (ii) The disc itself has angular velocity \(\dot\psi\) about a
vertical axis, which introduces small flapping and gyroscopic effects
on the blades; these are outside the thrust-only model. (iii) The
absolute rotor spin rate differs from the shaft-relative
\(\OmegaT\) by \(\dot\psi\), a fractional change of order
\(\dot\psi/\OmegaT\lesssim 1\%\) at the limits considered here, and
is neglected.

\subsection{Non-Dimensional Form}\label{ssec:inflow_nondim}

Dividing \eqref{eq:momentum} through by \(\rho\At(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\) converts
it to a relation between non-dimensional groups. With the inflow ratios
\(\lambda_i = \vi/(\OmegaT\Rt)\) and \(\lambda = (V_\infty+\vi)/(\OmegaT\Rt)\),
and the edgewise advance ratio \(\mu = V_\perp/(\OmegaT\Rt)\), the result is
\begin{equation}
C_T \;=\; 2\,\lambda_i\,\sqrt{\mu^2 + \lambda^2}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\lambda_i \;=\; \frac{C_T}{2\,\sqrt{\mu^2 + \lambda^2}}.
\label{eq:momentum_nondim}
\end{equation}
The square-root \(\sqrt{\mu^2+\lambda^2}\) is the non-dimensional
resultant velocity at the disc: \(\mu\) is the in-plane (edgewise)
component, \(\lambda\) the through-disc component, and their hypotenuse
is what the disc actually accelerates. Notice that
\(\lambda=\mu_z+\lambda_i\), where
\(\mu_z=V_\infty/(\OmegaT\Rt)\). Therefore the right-hand expression in
\eqref{eq:momentum_nondim} is generally an \emph{implicit} equation for
\(\lambda_i\), not a closed-form formula, because \(\lambda_i\) also
appears inside \(\lambda\).

\paragraph{Dimensional and non-dimensional forms in parallel.}
From here on we move between the dimensional induced velocity \(\vi\)
and the inflow ratio \(\lambda_i\) freely; they encode the same
physical quantity, related by
\(\lambda_i = \vi/(\OmegaT\Rt)\). The dimensional form is convenient
inside the dynamic-inflow ODE (Section~\ref{ssec:dyninflow}), the
non-dimensional form inside BEMT algebra, and the hover form \(v_h\) for
sizing arguments. A useful reading rule is:
\[
\lambda \quad\text{means total through-disc inflow, whereas}\quad
\lambda_i \quad\text{means the part caused by the rotor itself.}
\]

\subsection{Closing the Coupled System}\label{ssec:closing_coupled_system}

Equation~\eqref{eq:momentum_nondim} and the BEMT result
\eqref{eq:CT_BEMT} are two relations between the same three quantities
(\(\thetat\), \(C_T\), \(\lambda_i\), with
\(\lambda=\mu_z+\lambda_i\)). For a given pitch \(\thetat\) they can be
solved simultaneously for the steady-state inflow. In hover
(\(\mu=0\) and \(V_\infty=0\), so \(\lambda=\lambda_i\)), the two
equations reduce to
\begin{equation}
C_T
\;=\;
\frac{\sigma a}{2}\!\left(\frac{\thetat}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right),
\qquad
C_T \;=\; 2\lambda_i^2.
\end{equation}
Equating them gives
\begin{equation}
2\lambda_i^2
\;=\;
\frac{\sigma a}{2}\!\left(\frac{\thetat}{3}-\frac{\lambda_i}{2}\right)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\lambda_i^2 + \frac{\sigma a}{8}\lambda_i - \frac{\sigma a}{12}\thetat \;=\; 0.
\end{equation}
This is a quadratic in \(\lambda_i\). The two mathematical roots
represent two possible signs of induced flow; for the positive-thrust
hover branch we take the positive root:
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\lambda_i \;=\; \frac{\sigma a}{16}\!\left[
\sqrt{1+\frac{64\,\thetat}{3\,\sigma a}}\;-\;1
\right]
\quad\text{(hover, }\mu=0\text{)}.
\label{eq:lambda_implicit}
\end{empheq}
For small \(\thetat\) the bracket expands as
\(\tfrac{32\thetat}{3\sigma a} - \mathcal{O}(\thetat^2)\), giving
\(\lambda_i \approx 2\thetat/3\)---a useful order-of-magnitude check.
It also explains a feature that can otherwise look strange: near zero
pitch the BEMT term \(\thetat/3-\lambda_i/2\) cancels to first order, so
the hover thrust itself grows quadratically with pitch in this ideal
lossless model. Real rotors add profile drag, blade stall limits, and
finite root/tip corrections, so this exact small-pitch behaviour should
not be over-interpreted.

\subsection{Wake Inertia}\label{ssec:dyninflow}

Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda_implicit} gives the \emph{steady} inflow for a
given pitch, but it ignores time. In reality the wake is a column of air
with mass, and changing the rotor's thrust requires accelerating that
column---which cannot happen instantaneously. If at time \(t=0\) the
pilot steps the pitch up, the blade-element thrust jumps immediately to
its frozen-wake value (the wake hasn't moved yet,
so the local angle of attack is still \(\thetat-\phi\) at the old
\(\phi\)). Over the next few milliseconds the induced velocity builds
toward its new steady value, the local angle of attack drops, and the
thrust relaxes to its lower equilibrium. The rotor exhibits a
\emph{lead--lag} response in thrust because the inflow has \emph{lag}.

For control-design purposes the simplest faithful model treats the
uniform component of the induced velocity as a first-order state:
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\tau_i\,\dot{\vi} + \vi \;=\; \viss,
\label{eq:pittpeters}
\end{empheq}
where \(\viss\) is the steady-state induced velocity that solves
\eqref{eq:momentum} for the \emph{current} thrust \(\Tt\), and
\(\tau_i\) is the wake-relaxation time constant. This is the
\emph{one-state Pitt--Peters} dynamic inflow model. Equation
\eqref{eq:pittpeters} is the inflow analogue of an RC low-pass filter:
\(\viss\) is the target, \(\vi\) is the state, and \(\tau_i\) sets how
quickly the state chases the target.

The notation \(\viss\) deserves care. It does not mean a new physical
velocity in the wake; it means ``the value that \(\vi\) would eventually
settle to if the present thrust and flight condition were held fixed.''
For example, in hover \(\viss=\sqrt{\Tt/(2\rho\At)}\). In edgewise or
axial flow, \(\viss\) is defined implicitly by
\begin{equation}
\Tt
\;=\;
2\rho\At\,\viss\,
\sqrt{(V_\infty+\viss)^2 + V_\perp^2}.
\label{eq:viss_implicit}
\end{equation}
The dynamic model says that the real induced velocity \(\vi(t)\) lags
behind this steady target.

\subsection{The Pitt--Peters Time Constant}\label{ssec:pittpeters_tau}

What sets \(\tau_i\)? Two mechanisms contribute. The first is the simple
flow-traversal time: an air parcel takes roughly \(\Rt/v_h\) to cross
the rotor disc, so \(\tau_i\) must scale this way on dimensional
grounds alone. The second, more subtle effect is the
\emph{apparent mass} of the air column: when an impermeable disc is
accelerated through fluid, the fluid in its immediate neighbourhood is
accelerated with it, contributing an extra inertia even in the absence
of a true rotor wake. Lamb's classical potential-flow result gives the
apparent mass of a thin circular disc as \(\rho\,(8/3)\,\Rt^3\), a
fraction \(8/(3\pi)\approx 0.849\) of the mass of a sphere of radius
\(\Rt\). Pitt and Peters identified this same coefficient as governing
the lag of the uniform-inflow component, yielding
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\tau_i \;=\; \frac{8/(3\pi)}{4\,\OmegaT\,\lambda_i^{\text{trim}}}
       \;=\; \frac{0.849\,\Rt}{4\,v_h},
\label{eq:tau_i}
\end{empheq}
where the two forms are equivalent because, at trim,
\begin{equation}
\lambda_i^{\text{trim}} = \frac{v_h}{\OmegaT\Rt},
\qquad
\OmegaT\,\lambda_i^{\text{trim}}\Rt = v_h.
\end{equation}
The right-hand form is the easier to interpret: \(\tau_i\) is essentially
the time for an air parcel
travelling at the hover induced velocity to cross a quarter of the disc
radius, scaled by the apparent-mass coefficient. Higher disc loading
\(\Rightarrow\) faster \(v_h\) \(\Rightarrow\) shorter \(\tau_i\): hard-working
rotors have snappier wakes.

In \eqref{eq:tau_i}, \(\lambda_i^{\text{trim}}\) and \(v_h\) are trim
values, not instantaneous signals. This keeps the one-state model a
linear first-order lag around the selected operating point. For a
wide-envelope implementation one may update \(\tau_i\) slowly from the
current or commanded thrust, but it should be bounded away from zero
thrust because the formula becomes singular as \(v_h\to0\).

\paragraph{Caveat on Pitt--Peters time constants.}
The closed-form \eqref{eq:tau_i} is known to underpredict measured
inflow lag on full-scale rotors by a factor of 2--4. For RC tail rotors
it likely underpredicts somewhat less, but the value should be regarded
as a lower bound; see Section~\ref{sec:params}.

\subsection{The Coupled Model}\label{ssec:coupled_model}

Combining the blade-element thrust relation with the dynamic-inflow
state gives the complete tail-rotor model of this note:
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{align}
\Tt &= \rho\At(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\,\frac{\sigma a}{2}\!\left(\frac{\thetat}{3}-\frac{\vi+V_\infty}{2\OmegaT\Rt}\right),\label{eq:T_coupled}\\[4pt]
\Tt &= 2\rho\At\,\viss\sqrt{(V_\infty+\viss)^2+V_\perp^2},\notag\\[4pt]
\dot{\vi} &= \frac{1}{\tau_i}\bigl(\viss-\vi\bigr).
\label{eq:vi_coupled}
\end{empheq}
Equation~\eqref{eq:T_coupled} is BEMT, algebraic in \(\thetat\) and
\(\vi\). Equation~\eqref{eq:vi_coupled} is Pitt--Peters: a first-order
ODE in \(\vi\) driven by the instantaneous thrust through the
momentum-theory steady-state map. In hover this ODE can be
written explicitly as
\(\dot{\vi}=(\sqrt{\Tt/(2\rho\At)}-\vi)/\tau_i\); away from hover the
definition of \(\viss\) is implicit and is normally solved by a small
scalar iteration or by using the equivalent residual form in software.
The pair is a one-state nonlinear system with input \(\thetat\) and
output \(\Tt\), parameterised by the flight condition
\((V_\infty, V_\perp)\) and the rotor constants. This pair is inverted
in Section~\ref{sec:inverse}, analysed in the frequency domain---and characterised
across the operating envelope---in
Section~\ref{sec:linear}, and packaged for implementation in
Section~\ref{sec:impl}.

\section{Inverse Problem: Blade Pitch from Commanded Thrust}\label{sec:inverse}
% =====================================================================

The coupled model of Section~\ref{sec:inflow}---blade-element thrust
\eqref{eq:T_coupled} and dynamic inflow \eqref{eq:vi_coupled}---maps a
pitch input \(\thetat\) to a thrust output \(\Tt\). For control design
we need to run this map \emph{backwards}: given a commanded thrust
\(\Tcmd(t)\), what pitch \(\thetat(t)\) should we apply? The answer
will sit inside the tail-rotor inner loop as a feedforward block: the
outer loop (yaw control) produces a thrust demand, the inverse block
translates it into pitch, and the servo drives the tail-rotor pitch
mechanism.

This section derives the inverse in two steps. First, we invert the
blade-element relation algebraically to obtain the \emph{instantaneous}
pitch--thrust inverse, assuming the induced velocity is known
(Section~\ref{ssec:inst_inverse}). Second, we expand to the dynamic
case by carrying the induced velocity as a state, governed by the
Pitt--Peters ODE (Section~\ref{ssec:dynamic_inverse}). The result is a
complete nonlinear dynamic inversion of the rotor plant.
Section~\ref{sec:linear} linearises the system about hover trim,
characterises its lead--lag response in the frequency domain, and
quantifies the resulting inflow-softening factor across the
operating envelope; Section~\ref{sec:impl} then collects the
inverse into a compact form for direct implementation in an inner
control loop.

It is useful to separate two ideas before doing the algebra. The blade
element relation is an \emph{instantaneous map}: at the present value of
\(\vi\), it tells us what thrust a chosen pitch will produce right now.
The inflow equation is a \emph{state update}: it tells us how \(\vi\)
will move after the thrust command is applied. The inverse therefore
does not predict a future wake and then choose pitch from that future
wake. It uses the current wake estimate to choose the present pitch,
while the Pitt--Peters equation advances the wake estimate for the next
instant.

\subsection{The Instantaneous Inverse (Algebraic Step)}\label{ssec:inst_inverse}

The blade-element relation~\eqref{eq:linT} is linear in
\((\thetat, \vi, V_\infty)\) at fixed \(\OmegaT\), and therefore
trivially invertible for \(\thetat\). Solving \eqref{eq:linT} for the
pitch and setting the desired thrust to \(\Tcmd\) gives
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\thetat \;=\; \frac{6\,\Tcmd}{\rho\At(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\,\sigma a}
       \;+\; \frac{3\,(\vi+V_\infty)}{2\,\OmegaT\Rt}.
\label{eq:inv_instant}
\end{empheq}
This is the \emph{exact} inverse at every instant, conditional on
knowing the current \(\vi\) and \(V_\infty\): apply pitch
\eqref{eq:inv_instant} and the blade element will, instantaneously,
produce thrust \(\Tcmd\).

Two terms appear on the right-hand side. The first is the pitch the
rotor would need if no induced inflow were present---a naive ``pitch
per unit thrust'' calculation, with denominator \(\rho\At(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\,\sigma a/6\)
(the frozen-wake thrust-per-radian gain from
Section~\ref{ssec:dimensional}). The second is the extra pitch needed
to overcome inflow's stolen incidence: \(\vi\) and \(V_\infty\) together
set the inflow angle \(\phi\), and the blade must be pitched up by the
kinematic factor \(3/(2\OmegaT\Rt)\) of \eqref{eq:K2_K1_ratio} just to
retain its original effective angle of attack \(\alpha\).
Equation~\eqref{eq:inv_instant} is, in essence, the control-theoretic
restatement of the kinematic story from Section~\ref{sec:bemt}.

The word \emph{instantaneous} is important. The pitch in
\eqref{eq:inv_instant} compensates the inflow that exists now; it does
not use the steady-state inflow that will exist after the wake has
settled. Immediately after a thrust step, \(\vi\) is still close to its
old value, so the pitch command initially looks like a frozen-wake
inverse. As \(\vi\) evolves, the same formula automatically adds the
extra pitch needed to hold the commanded thrust against the stronger
induced flow.

\subsection{Expanding to the Dynamic System}\label{ssec:dynamic_inverse}

Equation~\eqref{eq:inv_instant} requires the \emph{current} value of
\(\vi\). On an RC platform \(\vi\) is not directly measurable: there
is no sensor that reports the induced velocity through the disc. The
inverse must therefore carry an internal estimate of \(\vi\),
propagated by the same physics that governs the real rotor's inflow---
namely, the Pitt--Peters ODE \eqref{eq:pittpeters} closed by the
momentum relation \eqref{eq:momentum}. The commanded thrust first
defines the steady inflow target \(\viss\):
\begin{align}
\Tcmd \;&=\; 2\rho\At\,\viss
\sqrt{(V_\infty+\viss)^2 + V_\perp^2},
\label{eq:viss_of_T}\\
\dot{\vi} \;&=\; \frac{1}{\tau_i}\bigl(\viss - \vi\bigr),
\label{eq:vi_dot}\\
\tau_i \;&=\; \frac{0.849}{4\,\OmegaT\lambda_i}, \qquad \lambda_i = \vi/(\OmegaT\Rt).
\label{eq:tau_of_vi}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:viss_of_T} is the momentum closure: it asks, ``if
the rotor were held at the commanded thrust and the flight condition
were fixed, what induced velocity would the wake settle to?'' In hover
this has the explicit solution \(\viss=\sqrt{\Tcmd/(2\rho\At)}\). With
axial or edgewise flow it is a scalar nonlinear equation for \(\viss\);
the positive working-state root is the one used here. Equation
\eqref{eq:vi_dot} is then the wake's relaxation: the current induced
velocity chases that target on the timescale \(\tau_i\). Equation
\eqref{eq:tau_of_vi} is the apparent-mass time constant from
Section~\ref{sec:inflow}, written in a scheduled form using the current
inflow estimate. A fixed-trim implementation can instead hold
\(\tau_i\) constant at the scheduled trim value.

Substituting the algebraic inverse \eqref{eq:inv_instant} for
\(\thetat\) and using the dynamic state \(\vi\) from
\eqref{eq:viss_of_T}--\eqref{eq:tau_of_vi}, we have a complete
\emph{nonlinear dynamic inversion}: a one-state nonlinear system whose
input is the commanded thrust and whose output is the pitch command.
No linearisation is involved; no operating point has been fixed; no
small-signal assumption has been made. The model is exact within the
BEMT and Pitt--Peters assumptions inherited from
Sections~\ref{sec:bemt}--\ref{sec:inflow}.

A subtle point: \(\viss\) in \eqref{eq:viss_of_T} appears to be driven
by \(\Tcmd\) (what we want), not by \(\Tt\) (what we produce). At a
control-theoretic level these are the same if the inverse is
working---\(\thetat\) is computed precisely so that \(\Tt = \Tcmd\) at
the blade element---so driving the inflow estimator from \(\Tcmd\) is
self-consistent and avoids needing a thrust measurement. The choice
does become relevant when servo or sensor delays are introduced, but
that is outside this section's scope.

Putting the pieces together, the continuous-time inverse has a simple
causal structure:
\[
\boxed{\text{current } \vi,\ \Tcmd,\ V_\infty,\ V_\perp}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\thetat \text{ from } \eqref{eq:inv_instant}}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\boxed{\dot{\vi} \text{ from } \eqref{eq:viss_of_T}\text{--}\eqref{eq:tau_of_vi}}.
\]
The pitch output uses the current state; the state derivative prepares
the wake estimate used by the next control instant. This is the same
division of labour used in the implementation form of
Section~\ref{sec:impl}.

\section{Transfer Function Analysis}\label{sec:linear}
% =====================================================================

The nonlinear plant \eqref{eq:T_coupled}--\eqref{eq:vi_coupled} of
Section~\ref{sec:inflow}---and its inverse derived in
Section~\ref{ssec:dynamic_inverse}---can be analysed in the frequency
domain by linearising about a chosen trim point. The resulting
transfer function exposes the pole and zero structure of the rotor,
the ``lead--lag'' character of its thrust response to a step in
pitch, and the inflow-softening factor that quantifies how much DC
gain is lost to the wake. This section closes by showing how this
softening factor varies across the operating envelope. The
linearised model is \emph{not} an alternative controller---the inner
loop will run the full nonlinear inverse, packaged for implementation
in Section~\ref{sec:impl}---but it gives the language in which the
plant's bandwidth and stability margins are usually discussed, and
is essential when an outer loop must be designed around this plant.

\subsection{Linearisation About Hover Trim}\label{ssec:lin}

\paragraph{Choosing trim.}
By \emph{trim} we mean an equilibrium operating point of the plant: a
constant pitch \(\btheta\) producing a constant thrust \(\bT\) with a
constant induced velocity \(\bvi\), all satisfying both BEMT and
momentum theory simultaneously. For a tail rotor on a hovering
helicopter the natural choice is the trim that balances main-rotor
anti-torque in still air. We specialise to hover (\(V_\infty=V_\perp=0\))
because the algebra is cleanest and because the tail rotor spends most
of its operating life near this condition; the non-hover case
generalises by replacing \(\bvi\) with the hover-equivalent
\(v_h=\sqrt{\bT/(2\rho\At)}\) and adjusting the trim accordingly.

\paragraph{Perturbation convention.}
Write each variable as a constant trim value plus a small perturbation:
\begin{equation}
\thetat = \btheta + \delta\thetat,\quad
\vi = \bvi + \delta\vi,\quad
\Tt = \bT + \delta T.
\end{equation}
We retain terms linear in the \(\delta\)-quantities and discard products
of two perturbations (assumed small\,\(\times\)\,small). The trim values
themselves satisfy the steady model and cancel out of the perturbation
equations, leaving a system in the \(\delta\)-variables alone.

\paragraph{What needs linearising.}
The blade-element relation \eqref{eq:linT} is \emph{already linear} in
its arguments at fixed \(\OmegaT\); reading off its coefficients gives the
perturbation form
\begin{equation}
\delta T \;=\; G_0\,\delta\thetat \;-\; \frac{3\,G_0}{2\OmegaT\Rt}\,\delta\vi,
\qquad
G_0 \;\equiv\; \frac{\rho\At(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\,\sigma a}{6},
\label{eq:bemt_perturbed}
\end{equation}
where \(G_0\) is the frozen-wake thrust-per-radian gain: the
high-frequency (instantaneous) response of thrust to a step in pitch
before the wake has time to react. The pitch and inflow coefficients
inherit the kinematic factor \eqref{eq:K2_K1_ratio} that separates them.
The nonlinearity is entirely in the momentum relation
\(\bT = 2\rho\At\bvi^{\,2}\), which couples thrust to induced velocity
through a square. Differentiating implicitly,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial T}{\partial \vi}\bigg|_{\text{trim}}
   \;=\; 4\,\rho\At\,\bvi,
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\delta\viss \;=\; \frac{1}{4\rho\At\bvi}\,\delta T
            \;\equiv\; k_v\,\delta T,
\label{eq:kv}
\end{equation}
where \(k_v\) is the linearised sensitivity of steady-state induced
velocity to thrust at the chosen trim:
\begin{equation}
k_v \;\equiv\; \frac{\partial \viss}{\partial T}\bigg|_{\text{trim}}
   \;=\; \frac{1}{4\rho\At\bvi}
   \;=\; \frac{\bvi}{2\bT},
\qquad\text{units: \si{\metre\per\second\per\newton}}.
\end{equation}
The two forms come from the same expression via the hover relation
\(\bT=2\rho\At\bvi^{2}\). Physically: \(k_v\) tells us \emph{how much
extra induced velocity each newton of additional thrust will pull
through the disc, in steady state}. It is large for low-thrust trims
(where \(\bvi\) is small and any new thrust must accelerate a slow wake
from near-rest) and small for high-thrust trims (where \(\bvi\) is
already substantial and incremental thrust matters less proportionally).

\paragraph{Linearised inflow dynamics.}
The Pitt--Peters ODE \eqref{eq:pittpeters} is already linear in \(\vi\)
at fixed \(\tau_i\). Substituting \(\viss = \bvi + k_v\,\delta T\) and
subtracting the trim equation \(\bvi = \bvi\) leaves
\begin{equation}
\tau_i\,\dot{\delta\vi} + \delta\vi \;=\; k_v\,\delta T.
\end{equation}
Taking the Laplace transform with zero initial conditions
(\(\dot{(\cdot)} \to s\,(\cdot)\)):
\begin{equation}
(\tau_i s + 1)\,\delta\vi(s) \;=\; k_v\,\delta T(s).
\label{eq:dvi_laplace}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Linearised Plant Transfer Function}\label{ssec:plant_tf}

Equations~\eqref{eq:bemt_perturbed} and \eqref{eq:dvi_laplace} are a
two-equation linear system in the three Laplace-domain quantities
\(\delta\thetat(s)\), \(\delta T(s)\), \(\delta\vi(s)\). Eliminate
\(\delta\vi\): from \eqref{eq:dvi_laplace},
\begin{equation}
\delta\vi(s) \;=\; \frac{k_v}{\tau_i s + 1}\,\delta T(s).
\end{equation}
Substitute into \eqref{eq:bemt_perturbed}:
\begin{equation}
\delta T(s) \;=\; G_0\,\delta\thetat(s)
\;-\;
\frac{3\,G_0}{2\OmegaT\Rt}\,\frac{k_v}{\tau_i s + 1}\,\delta T(s).
\end{equation}
Collect \(\delta T\), defining the dimensionless inflow--thrust coupling
that emerges from the algebra:
\begin{equation}
\beta \;\equiv\; \frac{3\,G_0\,k_v}{2\,\OmegaT\Rt}
   \;=\; \frac{\sigma a}{16\,\lambda_h},
\label{eq:beta_coupling}
\end{equation}
where the closed-form right-hand expression follows on substituting
\(G_0 = \rho\At(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\sigma a/6\) and the hover-trim sensitivity
\(k_v=1/(4\rho\At\bvi)\) from \eqref{eq:kv}, together with
\(\lambda_h=\bvi/(\OmegaT\Rt)\). Then
\begin{equation}
\delta T(s)\!\left[1 + \frac{\beta}{\tau_i s + 1}\right]
\;=\; G_0\,\delta\thetat(s),
\end{equation}
and rearranging to standard form,
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\frac{\delta T(s)}{\delta\thetat(s)}
 \;=\; \frac{G_0\,(\tau_i s + 1)}{\tau_i s + (1 + \beta)}.
\label{eq:plant_tf}
\end{empheq}
This is the linearised tail-rotor plant: thrust per radian of pitch
perturbation about hover trim, valid for small \(\delta\thetat\) over
a bandwidth limited by the linearisation.

\subsection{Reading the Plant: Lead--Lag Structure}\label{ssec:lead_lag}

The plant \eqref{eq:plant_tf} has one zero and one pole, both on the
negative real axis:
\begin{equation}
\text{zero at }s=-\frac{1}{\tau_i},
\qquad
\text{pole at }s=-\frac{1+\beta}{\tau_i}.
\end{equation}
Because \(\beta > 0\), the pole sits at a higher frequency than the zero.
The high-frequency and DC limits of the gain are
\begin{align}
\lim_{s\to\infty} \frac{\delta T}{\delta\thetat}
 &= G_0
 \qquad\text{(instantaneous, ``open-loop'' gain)},\\
\lim_{s\to 0} \frac{\delta T}{\delta\thetat}
 &= \frac{G_0}{1+\beta}
 \qquad\text{(steady-state, ``closed-wake'' gain)}.
\end{align}
\emph{The DC gain is lower than the instantaneous gain by a factor
\(1+\beta\).} A step in pitch produces an immediate thrust spike of size
\(G_0\,\delta\thetat\) (before the wake responds), which then relaxes
over a timescale \(\tau_i/(1+\beta)\) to the lower steady value
\(G_0\,\delta\thetat/(1+\beta)\). This is the \emph{lead--lag} response
described qualitatively in the introduction, now in closed form.

\paragraph{Physical reading of \(\beta\).}
The coupling \(\beta\) of \eqref{eq:beta_coupling} measures how strongly
induced inflow couples back into the thrust. Tracing its factors: the
kinematic ratio \(3/(2\OmegaT\Rt)\) is how much thrust each m/s of
induced velocity steals per radian of frozen-wake gain; \(k_v\) is how
much extra induced velocity each newton of thrust pulls through the disc.
Their product closes the loop and gives the fractional thrust loss
between the instantaneous and steady-state response:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\text{DC gain}}{\text{instantaneous gain}}
   \;=\; \frac{1}{1+\beta}
   \;=\; \frac{1}{1+\sigma a/(16\lambda_h)}.
\end{equation}
For typical RC tail rotors at hover trim, \(\beta\) sits in the range
\(0.5\)--\(1.5\)---meaning the DC gain is anywhere from \(40\%\) to
\(70\%\) of the open-loop gain. The thrust the rotor delivers in steady
state is significantly less than what its blade pitch suggests. This
phenomenon is \emph{inflow softening}, and Section~\ref{sec:linsoft}
rederives the same coupling \(\sigma a/(16\lambda)\) directly from the
closed BEMT--momentum system, confirming \eqref{eq:beta_coupling} and
extending it across the operating envelope.

\subsection{Inverting the Plant: The Lead Compensator}\label{ssec:compensator}

A feedforward compensator that cancels the plant dynamics is, by
definition, the inverse of the plant. Reciprocating \eqref{eq:plant_tf}:
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\frac{\delta\thetat(s)}{\delta T(s)}
 \;=\; \frac{\tau_i s + (1 + \beta)}{G_0\,(\tau_i s + 1)}.
\label{eq:comp_tf}
\end{empheq}
The roles of pole and zero are now swapped: the compensator has its
\emph{zero} where the plant had its \emph{pole}, and vice versa. The
compensator is, in classical control terminology, a \emph{lead
network}: its zero sits at a lower frequency than its pole, so its
phase contribution is positive across the intervening band. Cascading
\eqref{eq:comp_tf}~\(\to\)~\eqref{eq:plant_tf} gives the identity
transfer function. This is the linearised counterpart of the nonlinear
dynamic inverse derived in Section~\ref{ssec:dynamic_inverse}:
cancellation through the wake dynamics, expressed in the frequency
domain rather than as a state-update.

\subsection{The Inflow-Softening Factor}\label{sec:linsoft}

The DC gain \(G_0/(1+\beta)\) varies with the trim point because
\(k_v\propto 1/\bvi \propto 1/\sqrt{\bT}\). The factor \((1+\beta)\) is
the \emph{inflow softening denominator}; softening is strongest at low
thrust.

The coupling \(\beta\) was identified in \eqref{eq:beta_coupling} by
combining the BEMT perturbation with the momentum-derived sensitivity
\(k_v\). The same coupling can be derived more directly, without ever
linearising about an explicit trim, by closing the coupled
BEMT--momentum system in hover and differentiating the resulting
implicit relation. The two routes agree, as they must, and the second
makes the trim consistency manifest. In hover, the coupled equations
are
\begin{align}
C_T &= \frac{\sigma a}{2}\!\left(\frac{\thetat}{3}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right), \tag{BEMT}\\
\lambda &= \sqrt{C_T/2}. \tag{Momentum}
\end{align}
Eliminating \(C_T\):
\begin{equation}
\lambda^2 = \frac{\sigma a}{4}\!\left(\frac{\thetat}{3}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\right),
\end{equation}
with closed form
\begin{equation}
\lambda(\thetat) = \frac{\sigma a}{16}\!\left[\sqrt{1+\frac{64\thetat}{3\sigma a}}-1\right].
\label{eq:lambda_of_theta}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Trim sensitivity.} Differentiating implicitly:
\begin{equation}
2\lambda\,\frac{d\lambda}{d\thetat} = \frac{\sigma a}{4}\!\left(\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d\lambda}{d\thetat}\right)
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
\frac{d\lambda}{d\thetat} = \frac{\sigma a}{24\lambda + 3\sigma a/2}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\frac{dC_T}{d\thetat}\bigg|_{\text{steady}}
= \frac{\sigma a}{6}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{\sigma a}{16\lambda}}}_{S(\lambda)}.
\label{eq:dCT_dtheta}
\end{empheq}
The factor \(\sigma a/6\) is the instantaneous (high-frequency) gain
(non-dimensional form of \(G_0\)); \(S(\lambda)\) is the softening
factor. Comparing with the linearised form \(S = 1/(1+\beta)\) from
Section~\ref{ssec:lead_lag} confirms the closed form already given in
\eqref{eq:beta_coupling}:
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
\beta \;=\; \frac{\sigma a}{16\,\lambda_h},
\qquad \lambda_h = \sqrt{C_T/2}.
\label{eq:beta_consistent}
\end{empheq}
This is the expression to use in design. It depends only on the
aerodynamic group \(\sigma a\) and the trim inflow ratio
\(\lambda_h\)---no separate evaluations of dimensional gains are needed.

\subsection{Magnitude Across the Operating Envelope}\label{ssec:envelope}

For a 700 class tail rotor (\(\sigma=0.136\), \(a=5.7\), hover \(\bCT=0.0076\),
\(\blambda=0.062\)):

\begin{center}

\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule

\midrule

50\% hover  & 0.0038 & 0.044 & 1.12 & 0.47 \\
Hover       & 0.0076 & 0.062 & 0.79 & 0.56 \\
150\% hover & 0.0113 & 0.075 & 0.65 & 0.61 \\
200\% hover & 0.0151 & 0.087 & 0.56 & 0.64 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The DC gain varies by roughly \(40\%\) across the operating envelope. A
fixed-gain lead compensator will mis-track by this amount at the extremes.

\paragraph{Time constant varies too.}
The Pitt--Peters time constant
\(\tau_i = 0.849/(4\OmegaT\lambda)\) scales as \(1/\lambda\propto 1/\sqrt{C_T}\).
For the same 700 class rotor:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule

\midrule

Hover       & 3.3 \\
200\% hover & 2.4 \\

\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Both the gain and the time constant of the rotor depend on the trim
operating point, and both must be scheduled for a fixed-coefficient
controller to track across the envelope.

\section{Non-Dimensional Inverse and Minimum Parameter Set}\label{sec:nondim}
% =====================================================================

The dynamic inverse derived in Section~\ref{sec:inverse} is written in
dimensional form: its inputs are a commanded thrust in newtons, axial and
edgewise flows in m/s, and a yaw rate in rad/s; its output is a pitch
angle in radians; its internal state \(\vi\) is a velocity. In an actual
flight-control stack, however, the upstream yaw PID does not deliver a
thrust in newtons. It delivers a unitless command---typically in a
normalised range like \([0,1]\) or \([-1,1]\)---representing a fraction of
the available yaw authority. The thrust appears only as a fiction inside
the inverse, used to drive the BEMT and momentum algebra.

This section recasts the inverse in dimensionless form. The recipe is
standard. First, pick one reference scale---here, a reference thrust
\(T_{\text{ref}}\); every other quantity inherits a reference value
from it by the same physics that defined it in the dimensional inverse
(Section~\ref{ssec:nondim_refs}). Substituting these scaled forms into
each equation of the inverse, the dimensional constants cancel and the
inverse collapses to three update equations carrying just four
dimensionless coefficients \((A,B,C,D)\)
(Section~\ref{ssec:nondim_update}). Those four coefficients in turn
depend on only a handful of size-invariant dimensionless groups; those
groups, not the coefficients themselves, are what the firmware exposes
to the user---see Section~\ref{ssec:impl_exposed} in the implementation
chapter, where the mapping from exposed parameters to \((A,B,C,D)\) is
spelled out. The dimensionless update law below is the one an embedded
controller stores and runs each tick; Section~\ref{sec:impl} wraps it
in the I/O, state, and simplifications a practitioner needs.

\subsection{Reference Scales}\label{ssec:nondim_refs}

Choose a reference thrust \(T_{\text{ref}}\): the thrust to which the
dimensionless command \(u=1\) corresponds. The choice is the implementer's;
sensible candidates include the hover trim thrust \(\bT\), or some multiple
of it that brackets the available 3D authority. Once \(T_{\text{ref}}\) is
fixed, every other reference follows from it. The hover induced velocity
that would correspond to this reference thrust is
\begin{equation}
v_{\text{ref}} \;\equiv\; \sqrt{\frac{T_{\text{ref}}}{2\rho\At}},
\label{eq:nondim_vref}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding inflow ratio is
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{ref}} \;\equiv\; \frac{v_{\text{ref}}}{\OmegaT\Rt}.
\label{eq:nondim_lref}
\end{equation}
With these two scales fixed, every dimensional quantity in the inverse
has a natural dimensionless partner:
\begin{equation}
u \;\equiv\; \frac{\Tcmd}{T_{\text{ref}}},\quad
\hat{v}_i \;\equiv\; \frac{\vi}{v_{\text{ref}}},\quad
\hat{v}_{i,\text{ss}} \;\equiv\; \frac{\viss}{v_{\text{ref}}},\quad
\hat{V}_\infty \;\equiv\; \frac{V_\infty}{v_{\text{ref}}},\quad
\hat{V}_\perp \;\equiv\; \frac{V_\perp}{v_{\text{ref}}}.
\label{eq:nondim_vars}
\end{equation}
The pitch \(\thetat\) is already dimensionless (radians) and is carried
unchanged. Time can also be made dimensionless by referring it to the
characteristic wake timescale
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{ref}} \;\equiv\; \frac{0.849\,\Rt}{4\,v_{\text{ref}}},
\label{eq:nondim_tauref}
\end{equation}
which is the Pitt--Peters time constant from \eqref{eq:tau_i} evaluated at
\(\vi=v_{\text{ref}}\).

\subsection{The Dimensionless Update Law}\label{ssec:nondim_update}

Substituting \eqref{eq:nondim_vars} into the three equations of the
dynamic inverse---\eqref{eq:inv_instant}, \eqref{eq:viss_of_T}, and
\eqref{eq:vi_dot}--\eqref{eq:tau_of_vi}---each one simplifies.

\paragraph{Momentum closure.}
Equation \eqref{eq:viss_of_T} reads
\(\Tcmd = 2\rho\At\,\viss\sqrt{(V_\infty+\viss)^2+V_\perp^2}\).
Substituting \(\Tcmd = T_{\text{ref}}u = 2\rho\At v_{\text{ref}}^2\,u\) (by
\eqref{eq:nondim_vref}) and the velocity scalings, the prefactor
\(2\rho\At v_{\text{ref}}^2 = T_{\text{ref}}\) cancels from both sides:
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
u \;=\; \hat{v}_{i,\text{ss}}
\sqrt{(\hat{V}_\infty + \hat{v}_{i,\text{ss}})^2 + \hat{V}_\perp^2}.
\label{eq:nondim_momentum}
\end{empheq}
No rotor parameter appears: this is the same momentum closure for every
rotor. In hover (\(\hat{V}_\infty = \hat{V}_\perp = 0\)) the explicit
solution is \(\hat{v}_{i,\text{ss}} = \sqrt{u}\), the dimensionless
analogue of the hover formula \eqref{eq:vh}.

\paragraph{Pitch from command and state.}
The instantaneous inverse \eqref{eq:inv_instant} reads
\begin{equation*}
\thetat \;=\; \frac{6}{\rho\At(\OmegaT\Rt)^2\,\sigma a}\,\Tcmd
       \;+\; \frac{3}{2\,\OmegaT\Rt}\,(\vi+V_\infty).
\end{equation*}
Substituting \(\Tcmd = T_{\text{ref}}u = 2\rho\At v_{\text{ref}}^2\,u\)
and the velocity scalings, and using
\(\lambda_{\text{ref}}=v_{\text{ref}}/(\OmegaT\Rt)\):
\begin{equation}
\thetat \;=\; \frac{12\,\lambda_{\text{ref}}^2}{\sigma a}\,u
       \;+\; \frac{3\,\lambda_{\text{ref}}}{2}\,(\hat{v}_i + \hat{V}_\infty).
\end{equation}
Two dimensionless gains emerge:
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{align}
A \;&\equiv\; \frac{12\,\lambda_{\text{ref}}^2}{\sigma a},
\label{eq:nondim_A}\\[2pt]
B \;&\equiv\; \frac{3\,\lambda_{\text{ref}}}{2},
\label{eq:nondim_B}
\end{empheq}
giving the dimensionless pitch law
\begin{equation}
\thetat \;=\; A\,u \;+\; B\,(\hat{v}_i + \hat{V}_\infty).
\label{eq:nondim_pitch}
\end{equation}
\(A\) is the pitch (in radians) demanded per unit command when the state
and free-stream contributions are zero; \(B\) is the pitch demanded per
unit dimensionless through-disc velocity. The right-hand expressions in
\eqref{eq:nondim_A}--\eqref{eq:nondim_B} contain only \(\lambda_{\text{ref}}\)
and \(\sigma a\); air density, disc area, tip speed, and every other
dimensional constant of the rotor have cancelled. Moreover, since both
gains are built from the same two ingredients, they are not
independent: eliminating \(\lambda_{\text{ref}}\) between
\eqref{eq:nondim_A} and \eqref{eq:nondim_B} yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{A}{B^2} \;=\; \frac{16}{3\,\sigma a},
\label{eq:nondim_AB_relation}
\end{equation}
so the pair \((A,B)\) carries exactly the information of
\((\lambda_{\text{ref}},\sigma a)\)---one inflow-ratio number and one
blade-loading number.

\paragraph{Inflow state update.}
The Pitt--Peters ODE \eqref{eq:vi_dot} with the state-scheduled time
constant \eqref{eq:tau_of_vi} reads \(\dot{\vi}=(\viss-\vi)/\tau_i\) with
\(\tau_i = 0.849\Rt/(4\vi)\). Substituting \(\vi=v_{\text{ref}}\hat{v}_i\),
\(\viss=v_{\text{ref}}\hat{v}_{i,\text{ss}}\), and
\(\tau_i = \tau_{\text{ref}}/\hat{v}_i\) by \eqref{eq:nondim_tauref}, the
factor \(v_{\text{ref}}\) cancels from both sides of the time derivative
and we are left with
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{ref}}\,\dot{\hat{v}}_i
\;=\; \hat{v}_i\,(\hat{v}_{i,\text{ss}} - \hat{v}_i).
\label{eq:nondim_state_ode}
\end{equation}
If time is measured in units of \(\tau_{\text{ref}}\) (i.e.\
\(\tilde{t}\equiv t/\tau_{\text{ref}}\)) this equation becomes fully
parameter-free. Discretising with a control-loop period \(\Delta t\)
introduces a single dimensionless gain
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
C \;\equiv\; \frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{\text{ref}}}
   \;=\; \frac{4\,v_{\text{ref}}\,\Delta t}{0.849\,\Rt},
\label{eq:nondim_C}
\end{empheq}
giving the explicit-Euler update
\begin{equation}
\hat{v}_i \;\leftarrow\; \hat{v}_i \;+\; C\,\hat{v}_i\,(\hat{v}_{i,\text{ss}} - \hat{v}_i).
\label{eq:nondim_state}
\end{equation}
\(C\) is the only place the control-loop period \(\Delta t\) and the rotor
radius \(\Rt\) enter the inverse.

\paragraph{Yaw-rate contribution.}
Finally, the axial-flow composition \(V_\infty = V_\infty^{\text{aero}} +
\ell_t\dot\psi\) from \eqref{eq:Vinf_composition}, divided by
\(v_{\text{ref}}\), absorbs the geometric \(\ell_t\) into a scale factor
with units of seconds
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
D \;\equiv\; \frac{\ell_t}{v_{\text{ref}}},
\label{eq:nondim_D}
\end{empheq}
giving the dimensionless yaw-rate scaling
\begin{equation}
\hat{V}_\infty \;=\; \hat{V}_\infty^{\text{aero}} \;+\; D\,\dot\psi.
\label{eq:nondim_Vinf}
\end{equation}
The airframe airspeed component \(\hat{V}_\infty^{\text{aero}}\) either
arrives pre-non-dimensionalised from the airspeed estimator or is set
to zero for near-hover use.

\paragraph{Summary.}
The complete dimensionless inverse is the three update equations
\eqref{eq:nondim_momentum}, \eqref{eq:nondim_pitch}, and
\eqref{eq:nondim_state}, with the yaw-rate scaling \eqref{eq:nondim_Vinf}
on the input side. The stored constants are
\begin{equation}
A,\quad B,\quad C,\quad D
\label{eq:nondim_paramset}
\end{equation}
plus any constants used to non-dimensionalise the airspeed estimator's
output (typically zero on tail rotors without airspeed). Every other
physical parameter of the rotor and air has dropped out. Counting
independent content: \(A\) and \(B\) together carry two numbers
(\(\lambda_{\text{ref}}\) and \(\sigma a\), via
\eqref{eq:nondim_AB_relation}); \(C\) brings the rotor radius and the
loop period; \(D\) brings the moment arm.

\paragraph{The steady command--pitch curve.}
A useful by-product of the dimensionless pitch law is the steady
response in pure hover. Setting \(\hat{v}_i = \hat{v}_{i,\text{ss}} =
\sqrt{u}\) and \(\hat{V}_\infty = 0\) in \eqref{eq:nondim_pitch} gives
\begin{equation}
\thetat^{\text{steady}}(u) \;=\; A\,u \;+\; B\,\sqrt{u},
\label{eq:nondim_steady_curve}
\end{equation}
which depends on \((A,B)\) alone---\(C\) only enters in transients,
\(D\) only when the airframe yaws.

\section{Control Loop Implementation}\label{sec:impl}
% =====================================================================

The instantaneous inverse \eqref{eq:inv_instant} together with the
inflow model \eqref{eq:viss_of_T}--\eqref{eq:tau_of_vi} is a complete
nonlinear dynamic inversion of the tail-rotor plant. This section
collects those equations into a compact form suitable for direct
implementation in an inner control loop: first the small set of
exposed parameters a builder configures and the initialisation-time map from
those parameters to the four stored coefficients \((A,B,C,D)\)
(Section~\ref{ssec:impl_exposed}); then the live-tick separation of
fixed coefficients, inputs, state, and outputs
(Section~\ref{ssec:impl_ios}); then the update law in the form a
practitioner would actually code (Section~\ref{ssec:impl_update}); and
finally the simplifications a constrained embedded environment will
typically apply (Section~\ref{ssec:impl_simplifications}). The
transfer-function analysis of Section~\ref{sec:linear}---including the
envelope behaviour of the softening factor---informs how the resulting
controller will behave across the thrust range, but is not a
prerequisite for running the loop itself.

\subsection{Exposed Parameters}\label{ssec:impl_exposed}

Section~\ref{sec:nondim} reduced the loop to four dimensionless
coefficients \((A,B,C,D)\), but the firmware still has to choose those
numbers. Rather than expose the coefficients directly, we expose a
smaller set of more physically intuitive quantities and compute
\((A,B,C,D)\) from them at initialisation time.

We fix the implementer's choice of reference scale to
\(T_{\text{ref}} = \bT\), so that the dimensionless command \(u=1\)
corresponds to hover trim. Then \(\lambda_{\text{ref}} = \lambda_h\)
and \(v_{\text{ref}} = \bvi\), and the exposed parameters are three
dimensionless groups:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \(\btheta\) --- hover trim pitch. The tail-rotor blade pitch
      measured at hover with the heli's nominal loadout, in degrees,
      at the nominal governed headspeed \(\OmegaT\). Directly visible
      on telemetry. The knob that
      distinguishes a gently-loaded rotor from an
      aggressively-loaded one, replacing any intermediate
      aerodynamic quantity that would otherwise have to be computed
      or assumed.
\item \(\sigma a\) --- blade loading parameter. Lift produced per
      radian of pitch, normalised by disc area. Default
      \(\sigma a \approx 0.8\) for a two-bladed symmetric tail rotor;
      adjustment is only needed for non-standard blades.
\item \(\ell_t/\Rt\) --- boom-to-tail-radius ratio. How far behind
      the airframe yaw centre the tail sits, in tail radii. Default
      \(\ell_t/\Rt \approx 7\).
\end{itemize}
Typical ranges across the three RC size classes of
Section~\ref{sec:params}, at hover trim:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Quantity & 420\,mm & 550\,mm & 700\,mm \\
\midrule
\(\btheta\) [deg]   & 4--10          & 5--12          & 5--12          \\
\(\sigma a\)        & 0.6--1.0       & 0.7--1.0       & 0.7--0.85      \\
\(\ell_t/\Rt\)      & 4.5--7.5       & 5.4--8.0       & 5.9--7.4       \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The bands overlap heavily across all three classes; each
exposed parameter varies by under a factor of three across the
envelope. \(\btheta\) is directly observable on telemetry, \(\sigma a\)
is nearly fixed by blade type (two-bladed symmetric foil), and
\(\ell_t/\Rt\) by airframe convention---so in practice only
\(\btheta\) is set per flight, the other two from their defaults.

\paragraph{Mapping to the stored coefficients.}
With \(T_{\text{ref}}=\bT\), the reference scales of
Section~\ref{ssec:nondim_refs} collapse onto hover trim:
\(\lambda_{\text{ref}}=\lambda_h\) and
\(v_{\text{ref}}=\bvi=\lambda_h\,\OmegaT\,\Rt\). The four coefficients
\eqref{eq:nondim_A}, \eqref{eq:nondim_B}, \eqref{eq:nondim_C},
\eqref{eq:nondim_D} of Section~\ref{ssec:nondim_update} then depend on a
single rotor scalar \(\lambda_h\), given \(\sigma a\), \(\ell_t/\Rt\),
\(\OmegaT\), and the loop period \(\Delta t\). The firmware does not
measure \(\lambda_h\) directly; it derives it from \(\btheta\) by
inverting the steady command--pitch curve \eqref{eq:nondim_steady_curve}
at \(u=1\), which is exactly \(A+B=\btheta\). Substituting
\eqref{eq:nondim_A}--\eqref{eq:nondim_B} yields the quadratic
\((12/\sigma a)\,\lambda_h^2 + (3/2)\,\lambda_h - \btheta = 0\), whose
positive root is
\begin{equation}
\lambda_h \;=\; \frac{\sigma a}{24}\!
              \left(\,-\frac{3}{2}
              \;+\; \sqrt{\frac{9}{4} + \frac{48\,\btheta}{\sigma a}}\,\right).
\label{eq:nondim_lambda_h_from_theta}
\end{equation}
The stored coefficients then follow by substituting
\(\lambda_{\text{ref}}=\lambda_h\) and
\(v_{\text{ref}}=\lambda_h\,\OmegaT\,\Rt\) into
\eqref{eq:nondim_A}--\eqref{eq:nondim_D}:
\begin{equation}
A = \frac{12\,\lambda_h^2}{\sigma a},\quad
B = \frac{3\,\lambda_h}{2},\quad
C = \frac{\Delta t}{\tau_i}\ \text{with}\ \tau_i = \frac{0.849}{4\,\lambda_h\,\OmegaT},\quad
D = \frac{\ell_t/\Rt}{\lambda_h\,\OmegaT}.
\label{eq:nondim_ABCD_from_exposed}
\end{equation}
The firmware loop period \(\Delta t\) is a firmware constant, and
\(\OmegaT\) is the nominal governed rotor speed---a configuration-time
constant taken equal to the governor's setpoint, the same headspeed
at which \(\btheta\) was observed. Any unit conversion of \(\btheta\)
from degrees to radians happens before
\eqref{eq:nondim_lambda_h_from_theta}. The map
\eqref{eq:nondim_lambda_h_from_theta}--\eqref{eq:nondim_ABCD_from_exposed}
is evaluated once, at initialisation time, and \((A,B,C,D)\) are stored;
\(\OmegaT\) is not consulted again on the live control path. Runtime
deviations of the actual rotor speed from \(\OmegaT\) (governor sag,
transients) are analysed in Section~\ref{ssec:param_sensitivity}.

\subsection{Parameters, Inputs, State, Outputs}\label{ssec:impl_ios}

With the initialisation-time mapping of Section~\ref{ssec:impl_exposed} settled,
what remains is the live-tick specification: what arrives each tick,
what carries over between ticks, what is recomputed and discarded, and
what is emitted to the servo. In what follows, ``state'' means a value
carried from one control tick to the next; quantities such as \(\viss\)
and \(\tau_i\) are recomputed inside each tick and then discarded.

\paragraph{Fixed coefficients.}
\(A, B, C, D\) from Section~\ref{ssec:impl_exposed}. On the live
control path the dimensional rotor and air parameters
\(\rho, \At, \OmegaT, \Rt, \sigma, a, \ell_t\) appear nowhere; the loop
period \(\Delta t\) and the apparent-mass coefficient
\(\kappa=8/(3\pi)\approx 0.849\) have both been absorbed into \(C\).
If the loop rate changes, \(C\) must be recomputed.

\paragraph{Inputs} (provided each control tick):
\begin{equation}
u(t),\qquad
\hat V_\infty^{\text{aero}}(t),\qquad
\hat V_\perp(t),\qquad
\dot\psi(t)\quad\text{[rad/s]}.
\end{equation}
\(u\) is the dimensionless thrust command from the yaw outer loop,
normalised so that \(u=1\) corresponds to the reference thrust
\(T_{\text{ref}}\) used to define \(A\) and \(B\). It is taken positive
in the thrust-producing direction used throughout
Section~\ref{sec:inflow}. \(\hat V_\infty^{\text{aero}}\) and
\(\hat V_\perp\) come from the airframe's airspeed estimator,
non-dimensionalised by \(v_{\text{ref}}\) (or set to zero in near-hover
applications). The body yaw rate \(\dot\psi\) is taken from the same
gyro signal the outer yaw loop uses; together with \(D\), it gives the
axial-flow contribution discussed in Section~\ref{ssec:yaw_rate_axial}.
Inside the tick the inverse uses the composite
\begin{equation}
\hat V_\infty(t) \;=\; \hat V_\infty^{\text{aero}}(t) \;+\; D\,\dot\psi(t),
\label{eq:impl_Vinf_composite}
\end{equation}
which is the only place \(\dot\psi\) enters the algebra below.

\paragraph{State} (one scalar, evolved by the inverse block):
\begin{equation}
\hat v_i(t).
\end{equation}
\(\hat v_i\) is the current dimensionless induced-velocity estimate. It is
the only memory in the inverse block.

\paragraph{Algebraic quantities} (computed inside each control tick):
\begin{equation}
\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}(t).
\end{equation}
\(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\) is the dimensionless steady inflow target implied
by the current command and flight condition; it is recomputed each tick
and discarded.

\paragraph{Outputs}:
\begin{equation}
\thetat(t)\quad\text{[rad, to the servo]}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Update Law}\label{ssec:impl_update}

At each control tick of period \(\Delta t\), keep the order of ideas
straight. First, the command and flight condition define the steady
inflow target \(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\). Second, the current state
\(\hat v_i\), not \(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\), is used to compute the pitch
needed right now. Third, the discrete inflow-update coefficient \(C\)
sets how far \(\hat v_i\) moves toward \(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\) during
this tick:
\begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{align}
u \;&=\; \hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\sqrt{(\hat V_\infty + \hat v_{i,\text{ss}})^2 + \hat V_\perp^2},
   \label{eq:impl_viss}\\[2pt]
\thetat \;&=\; A\,u \;+\; B\,(\hat v_i + \hat V_\infty),
   \label{eq:impl_theta}\\[2pt]
\hat v_i \;&\leftarrow\; \hat v_i \;+\; C\,\hat v_i\,(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}} - \hat v_i).
   \label{eq:impl_visi_update}
\end{empheq}
These are the three boxed equations \eqref{eq:nondim_momentum},
\eqref{eq:nondim_pitch}, and \eqref{eq:nondim_state} of
Section~\ref{sec:nondim}, reordered into causal control-tick order.
The only implicit step is \eqref{eq:impl_viss}, a scalar solve for
\(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\). In hover it has a closed form
(\(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}} = \sqrt{u}\)); with axial or edgewise flow it
can be solved with a few Newton iterations because the positive
working-state branch is one-dimensional. The pitch equation
\eqref{eq:impl_theta} then uses \(\hat v_i\), not
\(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\), because pitch must cancel the inflow that
exists at the blade element now. The update
\eqref{eq:impl_visi_update} is what moves the estimate toward the future
steady value.

Near rotor spin-up or near zero commanded thrust, the state \(\hat v_i\)
itself appears as a factor in \eqref{eq:impl_visi_update} (it carries
the state-dependent Pitt--Peters time constant), so the effective lag
can grow without bound. A practical implementation therefore clamps
\(\hat v_i\) and \(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\) to small physically meaningful
lower bounds before applying the update.

\subsection{Common Simplifications}\label{ssec:impl_simplifications}

Several simplifications are commonly made when fitting the inner loop
into a constrained embedded environment.

\paragraph{Near-hover \(\hat V_\infty, \hat V_\perp \approx 0\).}
The momentum closure \eqref{eq:impl_viss} collapses on the
positive-thrust branch to
\begin{equation}
\hat v_{i,\text{ss}} \;=\; \sqrt{u}.
\end{equation}
This avoids the scalar solve and the airspeed estimate. The
simplification is sound for a tail rotor whose airframe spends most of
its time in near-hover or low-speed translation; the error grows with
edgewise flow and should be re-examined for operating points well
outside hover.

\paragraph{Axial-only flow \(\hat V_\perp = 0\).}
If in-plane flow is neglected but axial flow through the tail rotor is
kept, the momentum closure is no longer a general scalar solve. On the
normal working branch, where \(\hat V_\infty + \hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\ge 0\),
it reduces to a quadratic:
\begin{equation}
\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}
= \frac{\sqrt{\hat V_\infty^{\,2} + 4u} - \hat V_\infty}{2}.
\label{eq:impl_viss_axial}
\end{equation}
This expression reduces to the near-hover result when
\(\hat V_\infty=0\). It is useful when the implementation has a
reasonable axial-flow estimate (typically dominated by the yaw-rate
term \(D\,\dot\psi\)) but does not want to solve the full edgewise-flow
equation each tick. For strongly negative axial flow, the branch
condition should be checked and the guarded scalar solve in
\eqref{eq:impl_viss} should be used instead.

\medskip
\noindent
Equations \eqref{eq:impl_viss}--\eqref{eq:impl_visi_update}, together
with the four coefficients \(A, B, C, D\) of Section~\ref{sec:nondim},
are the deliverable of this note from a control-implementation
standpoint. Representative magnitudes for the underlying physical
parameters appear in Section~\ref{sec:params}.

\section{Parameter Selection and Sensitivity}\label{sec:params}

Section~\ref{ssec:impl_exposed} reduced the loop's tunables to three
exposed parameters: hover trim pitch \(\btheta\), blade loading
parameter \(\sigma a\), and boom-to-tail-radius ratio
\(\ell_t/\Rt\). The stored coefficients \((A,B,C,D)\) are derived
from them at initialisation time via
\eqref{eq:nondim_lambda_h_from_theta}--\eqref{eq:nondim_ABCD_from_exposed}.
This section answers three practical questions about those three
numbers: what values are typical across heli sizes
(Section~\ref{ssec:param_classes}), how sensitive the loop is to
error in each (Section~\ref{ssec:param_sensitivity}), and which
parameter to revisit when the airframe setup changes
(Section~\ref{ssec:param_retune}). A worked 700-class arithmetic
example closes the section (Section~\ref{ssec:worked_example}).

\subsection{Sensitivity to Parameter Errors}\label{ssec:param_sensitivity}

How tolerant is the loop to a wrong value in each stored parameter
(the three exposed parameters of Section~\ref{ssec:impl_exposed} and
the governor-sourced rotor speed \(\OmegaT\))?
Implicit differentiation of the static
quadratic~\eqref{eq:nondim_lambda_h_from_theta}, using
\(\partial f/\partial\lambda_h = (2A+B)/\lambda_h\) and writing
fractional sensitivities (elasticities) as ratios of logarithmic
differentials, gives the closed-form result
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\ln\lambda_h}{d\ln\btheta} \;=\; \frac{A+B}{2A+B},
\qquad
\frac{d\ln\lambda_h}{d\ln(\sigma a)} \;=\; \frac{A}{2A+B}.
\label{eq:lambda_elasticity}
\end{equation}
The two elasticities sum to
\((A+B)/(2A+B) + A/(2A+B) = (2A+B)/(2A+B) = 1\), reflecting that
\(\lambda_h\) is fixed by the product \(\sigma a\cdot\btheta\) up to
the quadratic correction. At the 700-class operating point of
Table~\ref{tab:exposed_class} (\(A=3.3\), \(B=5.3\)) the elasticities
evaluate to \(8.6/11.9=0.72\) and \(3.3/11.9=0.28\): a 10\% increase
in \(\btheta\) raises \(\lambda_h\) by 7.2\%, and a 10\% increase in
\(\sigma a\) raises it by 2.8\%.

Propagating these through~\eqref{eq:nondim_ABCD_from_exposed} (using
\(A\propto\lambda_h^2/\sigma a\), \(B\propto\lambda_h\),
\(C\propto\lambda_h\OmegaT\Delta t\), and
\(D\propto(\ell_t/\Rt)/(\lambda_h\OmegaT)\)) yields the fractional
sensitivities of the stored coefficients:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\(+10\%\) change in   & \(A\)      & \(B\)     & \(C\)     & \(D\)      \\
\midrule
\(\btheta\)           & \(+14\%\)  & \(+7\%\)  & \(+7\%\)  & \(-7\%\)   \\
\(\sigma a\)          & \(-4\%\)   & \(+3\%\)  & \(+3\%\)  & \(-3\%\)   \\
\(\ell_t/\Rt\)        & ---        & ---       & ---       & \(+10\%\)  \\
\(\OmegaT\)           & ---        & ---       & \(+10\%\) & \(-10\%\)  \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{What the table says.}
The first row is dominated by \(A\), which is quadratic in
\(\lambda_h\); the second row is muted because the response of
\(\lambda_h\) to \(\sigma a\) is roughly cancelled by the explicit
\(\sigma a\) in the denominator of \(A=12\lambda_h^2/\sigma a\). The
third and fourth rows are exact---\(\ell_t/\Rt\) and \(\OmegaT\)
enter only the dynamic coefficients \(D\) (and, for \(\OmegaT\), also
\(C\)) and do not perturb \(A\) or \(B\) at all. Crucially, the
\emph{hover static gain is invariant}: by construction \(A+B=\btheta\)
at \(u=1\), regardless of how \(\sigma a\) splits the sum between the
two coefficients and independent of \(\ell_t/\Rt\) or \(\OmegaT\).
Errors in the stored parameters therefore appear only off-hover (in
the curvature of the command--pitch map set by the \(Au\) term) and
in the dynamics (the inflow lag set by \(C\) and the yaw-rate axial
coupling set by \(D\)).

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \(\btheta\) is the most influential of the three user-tunable
parameters, but also the easiest to set correctly: it is observed
directly on telemetry during a stationary hover. A 10--20\% error is
unlikely once a single hover trim has been read.
\item \(\sigma a\) has weak effect. A coarse default of 0.75--0.80
covers all two-bladed symmetric-foil tail rotors to better than 5\%
on the stored coefficients, and the static hover relation is
unaffected. There is little reason to expose this knob to the user.
\item \(\ell_t/\Rt\) enters only \(D\), which is multiplied by
\(\dot\psi\). At small yaw rates the contribution is negligible; at
a 600\,\si{\degree\per\second} 3D pirouette
(\(\dot\psi\approx\SI{10.5}{\radian\per\second}\)) a 10\% error in
\(\ell_t/\Rt\) corresponds to roughly a 1~m/s error in the effective
axial flow, perturbing the operating point without destabilising the
loop.
\item \(\OmegaT\) is the nominal governed rotor speed, taken from the
rotor governor's configured setpoint rather than tuned independently.
It enters only the dynamic coefficients (\(C\) linearly, \(D\)
inversely) and leaves the hover static gain untouched. The standing
headspeed should be re-entered---and \(\btheta\) re-observed---if the
governor target changes by more than \(\sim 10\%\); runtime deviations
of the actual rotor speed from this stored value are analysed in the
dedicated paragraph below.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Runtime deviations of the actual rotor speed.}
The stored \((A,B,C,D)\) freeze \(\OmegaT\) at its nominal value.
The actual rotor speed deviates from this on at least two timescales:
brief headspeed sag during high-load segments (autorotation entries,
heavy collective pumps, sharp yaw demands) and slower drift in the
governor's standing target between flights. Writing
\(\OmegaT^{\text{act}} = (1+\epsilon)\,\OmegaT\) with \(\epsilon\) the
fractional deviation, three effects appear:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Frozen-wake gain.} BEMT thrust at fixed pitch scales as
\((\OmegaT\Rt)^2\) (eq.~\eqref{eq:linT}), so the actual rotor
delivers \((1+\epsilon)^2\approx 1+2\epsilon\) times the nominal
thrust for the pitch command the inverse produces. A 10\% sag is
therefore a \(\sim 20\%\) DC plant-gain shortfall---comparable to
the inflow-softening variation across the operating envelope
(Section~\ref{ssec:envelope}) and absorbed by the outer yaw loop's
integral action.
\item \emph{Inflow lag.} The wake-relaxation time constant
\(\tau_i = 0.849/(4\,\lambda_i\,\OmegaT)\) shortens roughly linearly
with \(\OmegaT^{\text{act}}\) (eq.~\eqref{eq:tau_i}), so the actual
\(\tau_i\) is \((1-\epsilon)\) times its nominal value. The stored
\(C\) over-discretises by the same fraction, slightly slowing the
state's chase of \(\hat v_{i,\text{ss}}\); the effect is small and
of correct sign in the lead--lag analysis.
\item \emph{Yaw-rate axial flow.} The dimensional contribution
\(\ell_t\dot\psi\) does not depend on \(\OmegaT\) at all, and \(D\)
was computed once with the nominal \(\OmegaT\); the stored
\(\hat V_\infty = D\,\dot\psi\) therefore mis-states the
non-dimensional axial flow at the actual operating point by the
fractional change in \(v_{\text{ref}}\) (itself proportional to
\(\OmegaT\) through \(v_h=\lambda_h\OmegaT\Rt\)), i.e.\ by
\(\epsilon\). This is a second-order correction on a
first-order term and is negligible against the dominant
\(\sigma a\)-driven uncertainty.
\end{itemize}
\noindent
In short: sustained RPM deviations show up primarily as a DC
gain shortfall of order \(2\epsilon\), recoverable only by re-running
the initialisation-time map with the new headspeed (or by re-observing
\(\btheta\) and reinitialising). Transient sag during a high-load segment
is a momentary plant-gain change that the outer yaw loop sees as a
short-duration disturbance; no inner-loop scheduling is required at
RC envelope amplitudes.

\subsection{Re-tuning When the Setup Changes}\label{ssec:param_retune}

The two routine triggers for a re-tune are a tail-blade swap and a
significant mass change. Both alter \(\btheta\); the first also
alters geometry. The remaining triggers (headspeed, boom length,
aerofoil) touch only one parameter each, often none.

\paragraph{Different tail blades (length or chord).}
A change in tail-blade length changes both \(\Rt\) (swept radius)
and \(\ell_t/\Rt\); a change in chord changes
\(\sigma=2c/(\pi\Rt)\), and hence \(\sigma a=2ca/(\pi\Rt)\). Both
quantities are geometric and can be read off the new blade
specification. Then hover and re-observe \(\btheta\) from telemetry:
a smaller disc requires a higher pitch for the same thrust, and
vice versa.

\paragraph{Heavier or lighter airframe.}
Tail thrust at hover scales with main-rotor reactive torque, which
in turn scales roughly as \(W^{3/2}\) through induced power. A 30\%
mass increase therefore raises required tail thrust by about 50\%
and \(\btheta\) by a similar fraction
through~\eqref{eq:nondim_lambda_h_from_theta}. Re-observe \(\btheta\)
after any significant payload change. \(\sigma a\) and
\(\ell_t/\Rt\) are unaffected.

\paragraph{Different headspeed.}
\(\OmegaT\) is a configuration-time constant set to the governor's nominal
target. Changing the standing headspeed requires updating that
constant and re-running the initialisation-time map---\(\lambda_h\) is
unchanged (it is derived from \(\btheta\)), but \(C\propto\OmegaT\)
and \(D\propto 1/\OmegaT\) shift linearly with it. The hover trim
pitch \(\btheta\) itself also shifts mildly---thrust at fixed pitch
scales as \(\OmegaT^2\), so a lower headspeed needs a larger
\(\btheta\) to deliver the same tail thrust---and should be
re-observed if the headspeed changes by more than
\(\sim 10\%\). Transient sag from the nominal value is left to the
outer loop (Section~\ref{ssec:param_sensitivity}).

\paragraph{Longer or shorter tail boom.}
\(\ell_t\) changes but \(\Rt\) does not. Update \(\ell_t/\Rt\) only.
\(\btheta\) shifts mildly in the opposite sense (a longer boom needs
less thrust at hover to balance the same main-rotor torque) and
should be re-observed.

\paragraph{Different tail aerofoil.}
Symmetric two-bladed foils all give \(\sigma a\) near 0.8 to within
\(\pm 10\%\). For non-standard cambered foils, recompute
\(\sigma a=(2c/\pi\Rt)\,a\) with the foil's actual lift-curve slope.
As the sensitivity table above shows, the loop is forgiving here.

\subsection{Worked Example: 700 class}\label{ssec:worked_example}

A representative 700-class electric machine (main rotor radius
\(\sim 0.79\)~m, all-up mass \(\sim 5\)~kg) carries the following
tail-rotor geometry:
\begin{align*}
\Rt &= 0.140~\text{m}, & c &= 0.030~\text{m}, & N_b &= 2,\\
\OmegaT &= 1036~\text{rad/s}, & \OmegaT\Rt &= 145~\text{m/s},\\
\sigma &= \tfrac{2\cdot 0.030}{\pi\cdot 0.140} = 0.136, & a &= 5.7,\\
\bT &= 12~\text{N}, & \At &= 0.0616~\text{m}^2, & \ell_t &= 0.95~\text{m}.
\end{align*}
Hence
\begin{align*}
\bvi &= \sqrt{12/(2\cdot 1.225\cdot 0.0616)} = 8.92~\text{m/s},\\
\tau_i &= 0.849\cdot 0.140/(4\cdot 8.92) \approx 3.3~\text{ms (Pitt--Peters)},\\
\sigma a &= 0.136 \cdot 5.7 = 0.778,\\
\lambda_h &= \bvi/(\OmegaT\Rt) = 8.92/145 = 0.0615,\\
G_0 &= \tfrac{1.225\cdot 0.0616\cdot 145^2\cdot 0.778}{6} = 206~\text{N/rad},\\
k_v &= 1/(4\cdot 1.225\cdot 0.0616\cdot 8.92) = 0.372~\text{m/(s\,N)},\\
\beta &= \frac{\sigma a}{16\lambda_h} = \frac{0.778}{0.984} = 0.79.
\end{align*}
The DC softening factor is \(S = 1/(1+\beta) = 0.56\): the steady-state
thrust delivered for a given blade pitch is about \(56\%\) of the
open-loop (frozen-wake) value.

\paragraph{Yaw-rate contribution.}
At a sustained 3D yaw rate of \(\dot\psi=\SI{600}{\degree\per\second}=10.5\)
rad/s, the axial-flow contribution from yawing alone is
\begin{equation*}
\ell_t\dot\psi \;=\; 0.95\cdot 10.5 \;=\; 9.95~\text{m/s},
\end{equation*}
slightly larger than the hover induced velocity \(\bvi=8.92\)~m/s
itself, and giving \(\mu_z=\ell_t\dot\psi/(\OmegaT\Rt)=0.069\) against
\(\lambda_h=0.0615\). In a with-thrust pirouette this axial flow
reduces the rotor's own \(\viss\) substantially; in an against-thrust
pirouette of the same magnitude the operating point approaches the
windmill-brake state and the smooth-wake momentum closure should be
treated with caution.

\section{Caveats}\label{sec:caveats}
% =====================================================================

The model developed in Sections~\ref{sec:bemt}--\ref{sec:impl} rests on
a chain of approximations: thin-foil aerodynamics, momentum-theory
inflow, a first-mode Pitt--Peters lag, a perfectly governed tip speed,
and an ideal pitch servo. Each is good enough to derive a working
inverse, but each has known limits that should be flagged before the
coefficients of Section~\ref{ssec:impl_exposed} are taken to the
bench. The points below are ordered roughly from those that most
directly perturb the stored coefficients to those that act outside the
inverse block.

\paragraph{Inflow time constant.}
Pitt--Peters time constants are systematically low compared to
flight-test data on full-scale rotors (factor of 2--4 underestimate);
RC practitioners often use \(\tau_i\approx 0.1\)--\(0.3\) rotor
revolutions empirically. For design, the Pitt--Peters value should
therefore be treated as a lower bound on the inflow lag, and either
inflated by a fitted multiplier or verified against bench data before
being used to set compensator coefficients. The coefficient \(C\) in
\eqref{eq:nondim_ABCD_from_exposed} inherits this same uncertainty and
is the natural place to absorb a fitted correction.

\paragraph{Low-Reynolds aerodynamics.}
Blade lift-curve slope drops noticeably at the low Reynolds numbers
(\(Re\sim\num{30000}\)--\num{80000}) typical of RC tail-rotor tips,
especially with thin symmetric sections, and section drag rises
correspondingly. The default \(\sigma a\approx 0.8\) of
Section~\ref{ssec:impl_exposed} assumes near-2D thin-airfoil
behaviour; for very small rotors or unusual sections the exposed
\(\sigma a\) should be scaled down accordingly.

\paragraph{Blade planform.}
Solidity calculations as written assume rectangular blades. Tapered or
paddle blades need an equivalent solidity weighted by \(r^2 c(r)\),
which concentrates the lift contribution toward the tip and slightly
raises the effective \(\sigma a\) relative to a chord-averaged
estimate.

\paragraph{Main-rotor wake.}
The tail rotor sits inside the main-rotor wake, so \(V_\perp\)
in~\eqref{eq:momentum} is dominated by main-rotor downwash---the main
source of tail-rotor effectiveness loss in turns---and not by airframe
sideslip alone. A simple aero airspeed estimator does not see this
contribution. The pragmatic options are to augment \(\hat V_\perp\)
with a main-rotor thrust-dependent term, or to set
\(\hat V_\perp\equiv 0\) (the near-hover simplification of
Section~\ref{ssec:impl_simplifications}) and absorb the lost authority
into the outer loop's headroom.

\paragraph{Yaw-rate axial flow.}
The axial flow \(V_\infty\) is dominated in 3D flight by the yaw-rate
contribution \(\ell_t\dot\psi\) rather than by the airframe-translation
component \(V_\infty^{\text{aero}}\)
(Section~\ref{ssec:yaw_rate_axial}); in slow forward flight or
sustained hover the two contributions can be of comparable magnitude.
The \(D\,\dot\psi\) term \eqref{eq:impl_Vinf_composite} captures the
dominant component for free; the airspeed-based correction is optional
and only earns its keep on airframes that fly meaningful translational
speeds.

\paragraph{Moment-arm geometry.}
The moment arm \(\ell_t\) is the geometric distance from the airframe
yaw centre (close to the CG) to the tail-rotor hub. On machines with
relocatable batteries or significant fuselage mass changes it shifts
slightly with loadout, which propagates linearly into \(D\). Treating
it as fixed is acceptable for typical RC loadouts, where the shift is
a few percent; competition pilots who change battery position between
disciplines may wish to expose a per-setup override.

\paragraph{Governor and pitch servo.}
Two unmodelled blocks bracket the inverse derived here. The upstream
governor holds \(\OmegaT\) constant only within its own loop
bandwidth; the inverse takes \(\OmegaT\) as a fixed parameter at the
governor's nominal target, so transient sag from that target shows up
as a mismatch between the stored coefficients and the actual plant
(Section~\ref{ssec:param_sensitivity}). Downstream, the
commanded \(\thetat\) reaches the blade root only through a servo and
linkage with their own bandwidth, deadband, and possible backlash. The
inverse is exact in the limit that both blocks are ideal; deviations
show up as a plant-gain shift at the input of the inner loop and as a
static error at its output, respectively, and are best characterised
empirically on the airframe rather than modelled from datasheets.
